SCINTILLA · SIGMA HISTORY · 24 SEPTEMBER 2026

Two years of unusual days, for every name — and what happened after them

A sigma is one sentence: this name moved further today than it usually moves. Until now the Hub only remembered the ones it caught live, from 23 September 2026. This page answers where the number comes from, where it is kept, and what the two years before that look like when the same rule is walked backwards over 364 names — 12,198 unusual days across 486 trading sessions.

"Are you capturing this going backwards for other companies? Because like, how? How are you registering the sigma? Against what is it computing? Where is it being saved? Is it on database? How is it going to work?" — Alan, 24 September

THE FOUR ANSWERS, IN ORDER

1 · How a sigma is registered

Every ten minutes in market hours, and once more after the close, a small program reads the day's price for all 364 names and asks two questions about each. Is this move big compared with this name's own usual day? And is it simply big? If either is true, that name had a sigma day, and one row is written. Nothing else happens: no ranking, no opinion, no trade.

2 · Against what it is computing

Against the name's own usual day — the spread of its last 60 daily moves, which the Hub calls the heartbeat and stores in public.ticker_heartbeat_daily. A 3% day is nothing for a name that normally swings 4%, and enormous for one that normally moves 0.4%. The bar is set per kind of thing, because a whole market moving is not the same event as one company moving:

data/scintilla-rules.json, version 2026-09-24.1. Change a number there and the detector, the backfill and the Hub all follow it.

The one rule that keeps it honest: the usual day used is always the one measured before the day being judged. If today's own move were inside the divisor, a wild day would quietly raise the bar it has to clear, and judge itself.

3 · Where it is saved

In the database, in one table: public.scintillas. One row per name per day, carrying the move, the usual day it was divided by, which rule fired, and the inputs — so any row can be recomputed by hand. The key of a row is price_outlier|SYMBOL|DATE, which is why the same day can never be stored twice, whether it was caught live or filled in afterwards.

4 · How it works going forward

Unchanged: the live pass keeps writing today's rows every ten minutes. What is new is a second door into the same room — ?mode=backfill — which walks stored daily bars from 2024-10-15 to today and writes the days nobody was watching, through the same rule, the same key and the same table. It goes a slice of names at a time and can be stopped and resumed; running it twice leaves one row, not two.

The worked example — EOSE, 2026-09-23

MOVE

6.50% — the spread of its last 60 daily moves, as of the session before

11.82% ÷ 6.50% = 1.82× its usual day, downwards

EOSE is the case that shows why two families exist. It is a wild name — its ordinary day is 6.50% — so an 11.82% fall is only 1.82× its usual day and the statistical rule alone would have said nothing. The raw floor caught it. Its forward return is not in this study: 2026-09-23 is the last session in the data, so the next 20 closes do not exist yet. That is left blank rather than filled with a guess.

THE BACKFILL, AND WHAT IT PRODUCES

The plan is deliberately boring: the same function set, one new mode. ?mode=backfill&from=&to=&symbols=&cursor= reads stored daily bars, asks the live detector the same question for each past session, and inserts with ignore-duplicates. A backfilled row is marked backfilled: true and timestamped at that session's own close. A live row is never displaced. The migration is additive — one index and one counting view — and has an exact rollback.

The two small differences are the edge of the window: the two runs start their 60-session count one bar apart, which moves one borderline day in and out.

Across all 364 names the rate is 25.1 a day over the full two years, and 21.08 a day over the most recent 60 sessions. The rules file's note — "expect roughly three times these counts across all 364 names", about 21 — is right for today and a little low for the two-year window, which contains April 2025.

WHAT HAPPENED AFTER — THE PLAYBOOK'S FIRST CHAPTER

For every stored event, the return over the next 1, 5 and 20 sessions, measured only from closes that existed after the event, and the same return minus SPY over the same dates. The honest answer first:

A sigma day, on its own, does not tell you what comes next. Across all 11,918 events with a 20-session answer, the median return against SPY is +0.06% and 50.4% of them beat SPY — a coin toss. At one session it is +0.01% (50.2% beat), at five +0.12% (51.1% beat). Of the 15 groups below, one has an interval clear of zero, which is about what pure chance produces when you cut the same data 15 ways.

BREADTH OF THE UNUSUAL

The same events counted a different way: how many names had a sigma day, each day, up and down separately. This is a market-wide series of its own, and it is the one place in this study where the shape is unmistakable — the market's worst days are not a few big movers but nearly everything moving at once.

Weakly, and not in a straight line. The quietest fifth of days is followed by the best 20 sessions (+2.43%, higher 78.5% of the time) and the widest fifth by +1.43%, but the middle buckets do not line up in between, and every bucket is positive because the market rose over this window. This is not a timing rule. It is a description.

The Geiger comparison is not in this page, and here is why. The chart API serves one current Geiger artifact; no stored history of it exists that this lane can read, and there is no database key in this environment. Lining breadth up against the Geiger needs the Geiger's own daily value kept the way the heartbeat now is. That is a small table and a nightly write — worth doing, and named in the decisions below — but it cannot be faked from today's single reading.

WHAT COULD BE WRONG

  • Survivorship, the big one. The 364 names are today's universe. Anything that was delisted, taken private or collapsed in the last two years is absent — and those names had the wildest sigma days of all. Every forward return here is therefore measured on companies that made it to today, which flatters them. The honest fix is a point-in-time universe, which the Hub does not have yet.
  • The usual day was recomputed, not read. The store has heartbeat rows from 2024-09-18, but no key to that table exists in this environment, so this study computed each name's 60-session spread from bars that close before the session — the same formula the store uses. The backfill itself, when the coordinator runs it, will prefer the stored row for the prior date and fall back to this only where a row is missing, saying which it used in every row.
  • Prices are adjusted as they stand today. A split or a large dividend rewrites old bars, so a two-year-old "move" is measured on today's adjusted series, not on what printed that afternoon.
  • Fifteen groups, one hit. DOWN · index_etf is the only interval clear of zero. Cut any data fifteen ways and one will look special; treat it as a question, not a finding.
  • The window is one market. 2024-10-15 to 2026-09-23 contains April 2025 and a long rise. Two years is a small sample of regimes, not a law.
  • Events near the end have no answer. 280 events are too recent to have 20 sessions after them, including the EOSE example. They are left blank, never zero.

WHAT I DID NOT DO

  • Nothing was deployed and nothing was run against the database. The migration, the new mode and the tests are written and tested locally; the coordinator applies the migration and runs the backfill.
  • No row was written to public.scintillas by this lane — the counts above come from replaying the rule over locally cached bars, not from the table.
  • No price table was touched, and no credential was read or printed.
  • Earnings and economic sigmas are not backfilled — only price. Their history lives in tables this lane cannot read from here.
  • No trading rule is proposed. The measurement says a sigma alone does not predict; the Playbook's next chapter is what a sigma plus something else does.
