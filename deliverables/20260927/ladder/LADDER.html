The ladder: if I bought at this RSI level, or at this average, how did it go?

S8 · 27 Sep 2026 · built 2026-09-27 18:00 UTC · forward outcomes only · price only · nothing here predicts

  1. Start with the market, not the stock. A stock's own RSI at the bottom 5% of its 3-year history was up 20 sessions later 59.4% of the time, against 56.4% for any day, and it beat SPY over the same days only 51.6% of the time — it mostly rode the tide. By the bottom 30% the edge is gone (56.4%).
  2. For SPY itself a low RSI buys a bigger bounce, not better odds — except in an uptrend. SPY RSI in its bottom 10% while SPY was above its 200-day: up 74.8% of 103 times (any uptrend day 67.2%; right at the 74.8% that random uptrend days reach 1 time in 20). The same RSI below the 200-day: 54.3%, a coin flip. The first cut's “more than 3% above the 200-day” version checks out: 84.2% of 57 (48.5% when more than 3% below).
  3. “Extended” is not the danger for SPY; being far below the 200-day is. SPY in the top 10% of its own distance above the 50-day was up 71.6% after 20 sessions (any day 65.8%). Small caps are the exception: IWM that stretched, 52.1% (any day 60.0%). SPY in the bottom 10% of its distance to the 200-day: 52.4%.
  4. The best combination that held in both halves of history: the stock's RSI in its bottom 20%, while the stock is still well above its own 200-day, and the market in this state: SPY RSI in its bottom 30%. Up 67.0% in 2004–2016 and 66.4% in 2017–2026, against 56.5% and 56.4% for any day — but only 231 trades in 85 entry months, so it is a rare setup, not a daily one.
  5. Into earnings (timing now corrected), the stock's RSI adds almost nothing: the 20-session run to the last close before a report was up 60.6% of 18,504 times; with SPY's RSI in its bottom 30% at the start, 63.3%. NVDA's last 12 run-ups were all up (70.0% over its whole history) — that is today's NVDA, not a rule. COST's low-RSI entries before a report went 27.3% up in the last five years.

How to read every table

  • RSI at or below X%: RSI(14) (Wilder's) ranked against the fund's or stock's own previous 3 years (756 sessions, at least 250 readings). “Bottom 5%” means lower than 95% of its own readings over the past 3 years. Today's reading is never inside its own sample.
  • The averages are the Station cloud lines exactly (station-clouds.js): 13- and 21-day EMAs started at the first close, weight 2/(n+1), not used for the first 60 sessions; 50- and 200-day simple averages. Distance is counted in usual days (the % gap ÷ the instrument's own 60-session spread of daily moves). The bands (bottom 10%, 10–25%, …, top 10%) are the distance's rank in its own previous 3 years, so every instrument gets its own bands; each cell says what the band meant in usual days and %. At the average = within ½ usual day of it.
  • Episode = the first day of an unbroken run of days the condition is true (a week of low RSI counts once). Non-overlapping trades = the first signal, then the next one only after the holding period is over. Entry and exit are closes. No costs.
  • Up after 20 = the close 20 sessions later is above the entry close. Middle = the median result. Trend on = the instrument closed above its own 200-day that day.
  • 95% range = a bootstrap that resamples whole calendar months, because sell-offs make many days (and many names) fire together. Shuffle 95th = draw the same number of random days from the same instrument (and, for stocks, the same market state) 200 times; a share above the 95th of those draws is not luck of the draw.
  • Recency columns: full history · last 5 years (entries from 2021-09-27) · last 250 episodes (funds) or each stock's own last 12 episodes / last 12 reports (stocks). Last 8 = how many of the latest 8 episodes went up (funds, single stocks); for pooled stocks, in how many of the last 8 calendar years the condition beat that year's any-day line.

1 · First, the report-timing fault — fixed

The earnings export had a before-open / after-close flag on only 840 of 21,624 reports. S6 treated a blank as “after the close”, so for a morning reporter (JPM, WMT, HD…) the “run-up” quietly included the report-day reaction. FMP's calendar was not used: its key is not in this machine's environment, and the brief allows the environment only. So every missing time was inferred and flagged in a new file, research/statistics/data/earnings-export-timed-20260927.json:

  • The rule: a report before the open moves the overnight gap into the report date, and one after the close moves it into the next session. When one gap is at least twice the other and at least one usual day, that decides it (9,473 reports). When neither gap is clear, the name's own clear calls within 3 years decide (9,202). A report dated on a weekend or holiday reacts on the next session either way (98). Still unknown: 2,011 — these are left out of the run-to-report study.
  • Checked against the 826 reports whose time the export does carry: where the gap rule made a call, it agreed 95.3% of the time (491 right, 24 wrong, 308 left to the neighbour rule).
  • What changed in S6 (all names pooled): 10,240 reports moved to the earlier news session. The run-up was up 59.8% → 60.4%, middle +1.79% → +1.73%. The report-day move (middle size, either direction) grew from 1.8% to 2.8% — the reaction now lands on the report day, where it belongs. 6 · What could be wrong

  • Many tries. 1,159 combinations qualified for the both-halves test, and more were looked at. With that many tries, some will look good by chance. Requiring both halves and checking the shuffle baseline cuts this down but does not remove it. Treat the top 5 as candidates, not rules.
  • Survivors only. the names are today's universe (the earnings export's tickers that have bars); companies that were delisted, merged or dropped are not in it, so pooled name results describe survivors and lean optimistic.
  • Price only. price only: the chart API serves split-adjusted, not dividend-adjusted bars, so every return here leaves out dividends (about 1.5–2% a year for SPY, less over 20–40 sessions: ≈ 0.1–0.3%).
  • Report times are inferred for 96.1% of reports (95% agreement where they could be checked). A wrong time moves one session between the run-up and the report day.
  • Clustering. Stock entries bunch into the same sell-offs. The month-clustered ranges are wide for exactly that reason — a combination seen in “200 trades” may be only 40 or 50 separate months.
  • Regimes. A ranking over the previous 3 years adapts, but 2004–2026 holds only a handful of bear markets, and funds like SPY spent 78% of days above their 200-day, so the “trend off” rows are thin.
  • QQQ starts in 2011 (see above). XLC starts in 2018 and XLRE in 2015, so their rows are short.

What I did not do

  • No FMP call: its key is not in this machine's environment. No database write, no deploy, no push. No paid model API.
  • No costs, slippage, stops or position sizing. Exits are fixed (10/20/40 sessions, or the last close before the report).
  • No intraday data; entries and exits are daily closes.
  • The S6 page and its runup-into-earnings.json were left as they were. The corrected numbers are in data/s8-timing.json and summarised in part 1.
