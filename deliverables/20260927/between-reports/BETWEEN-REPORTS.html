Between two reports: what was true at the real lows

CORRECTION · 27 Sep, after review. Three things on this page are wrong or misleading:
1. "The post-report drop" (13.5% of lows on the first two days after a report) is not real. Randomly shuffled prices put the low on those days 14.0% of the time. Any wandering price line tends to have its lowest point near the start or end of a stretch.
2. "Lift" at the lows is circular for RSI. A low RSI comes with a falling price by definition. The question that matters runs forward: when the RSI is low, what happened next?
3. Report times were mostly missing (96% of rows). So for companies that report before the open (JPM, WMT, HD…), the "exit before the report" was really the report-day close.
Replaced by a forward study (S8): every day a condition holds, what happened 10, 20 and 40 sessions later, and into the report, measured against SPY. It uses an RSI ladder from the bottom 5% to 70%, the four cloud averages, and the market state first. Keep the tables below only as the record of the first pass.

This time the data picked the conditions. At the lowest close before each run into a report, a few things kept showing up. The stock's RSI was at the bottom of its own range. The close sat 3 or more usual days under its 13- or 21-day EMA. SPY was oversold too. And, surprisingly often, it was the first two sessions after the previous report. Rules built from these reached 65% winners in both halves of history (10 of them). None reached 70%. They also fire in only a few dozen market sell-offs.

1 · The stretch between two reports

  • A stretch runs from the first close after report k's news session to the last close before report k+1's news session. The news session is the first session that trades on the report (S6's rule: a report before the open → that day; after the close → the next day). A stretch is kept when it holds 20 to 90 closes. A longer gap means a report is missing from our list.
  • Only names with at least 8 listed reports in their own price history count. The history starts after the name's last hole of more than a year, which is the S6 rule.
  • A typical stretch holds 62 sessions. Buying the first close after a report and selling at the last close before the next one was up 60.3% of the time, with a middle return of +2.97%. That is the benchmark every rule below has to beat.
  • The best entry in hindsight is the lowest close of the stretch. It comes a median of 24 sessions after the report (half of them fall between 7.0 and 45.0). From there to the last close before the report, the middle gain was +10.58%. Holding through the report added little: +10.89% to the report close, +11.33% five sessions later.
  • Swing lows and highs are every low (or high) that is the extreme of the 5 sessions on each side of it. There are 71,359 swing lows and 70,221 swing highs.

The lows and highs are picked with hindsight on purpose: they mark where the turn really came. Every factor, though, is read only from the bars up to that close, so it is something you could have seen on the day.

What the data says:

  • The strongest signs of a low were an RSI at the very bottom of the stock's own 3-year range, a close 3 or more usual days under the 13- or 21-day EMA, a close under the straight-line channel, and SPY oversold. Each showed up roughly 4 to 8 times more often at the hindsight low than on an ordinary day.
  • Swing highs are the mirror image: own RSI at the top of its range, a close above the straight-line channel, and a close within one usual day of the 60-session high.
  • The first two sessions after a report are the hindsight low 13.5% of the time, against 3.3% of the days. That is the post-report drop. (Wrong: shuffled prices give 14.0%. See the correction at the top.) At ordinary swing lows the early sessions are not special (lift 0.82×). So this is about the stretch's single deepest point, not about turns in general.
  • The clouds: the lows sit mostly under red fast clouds (--+, ---, about 2×). Under all-green clouds (+++) a hindsight low shows up at 0.4×, less than half as often as on an ordinary day.
  • The parallel channel tells the same story more weakly than the straight-line channel. Below the lower rail the lift is 2.91×, against 4.88× below the straight-line channel. Part of the reason is that it exists on fewer days.

3 · Rules the data suggested, and how they traded

How the rules were found. Every “below X” and “at or above X” cut of every factor, and every cloud state, was scored by its lift at the hindsight low. Only 2003–2016 was used for this. A cut had to cover between 3% and 50% of ordinary closes. The 12 with the biggest lift (at most two from any one family, such as the two short EMAs) form the pool. The trade is:

  • Entry at the close of the first session in the stretch where every part of the rule is true on that same session. If that never happens, there is no trade.
  • Exit (main) at the last close before the report — (corrected: for companies reporting before the open this was the report-day close; report times were missing) Other exits: the report session's close, and the close five sessions after it (to see “sell the news”).
  • “Up” means the exit close is above the entry close. No costs, no slippage, no stop.

How to read it — and why the flags need care:

  • Every one of the 10 flagged rules needs SPY oversold (its RSI in its own bottom 10%), and 8 of them also need the first two sessions after a report. In practice that means: buy a name that sold off on its report while the whole market is washed out, and hold until the next report.
  • Because the market has to be oversold, the trades bunch on the same few weeks: Aug 2011, Oct 2008, Oct 2018, May 2022, Oct 2023. From 2017 onward each flagged rule enters in only 28 to 45 distinct months. Its three busiest months carry up to 42% of its trades. So a “200-trade” rule is really a few dozen market episodes. The ± range in the table treats every trade as independent, so it is too narrow.
  • If every month counts once, the flagged rules still win 62% to 71% of the time from 2017 onward. The pattern held across both halves. But it is thin, and none reached 70% in the later half. The best, d50_ud < -6 + spy_pct < 10 + since < 3, went from 75.4% (2003–16) to 68.6% (2017→).
  • Holding through the report changed little for these rules (the last two columns).

Where each number comes from

  • Prices: the finished daily bars that the chart API (scintilla-massive-chart-api.fly.dev) served on 27 Sep. S7 saved them, and this study reused that same bar cache (332 files, including SPY). Nothing was fetched again and nothing was written to any database.
  • Report dates and times: research/statistics/data/earnings-export-with-estimates-20260926.json, the same export S6 and S7 used. Sizes: meta-cohorts-caps-20260926.json.
  • Code: research/statistics/between.mjs (the factors and rules), s7b-between.mjs (the run), s7b-page.mjs (this page). Every number above is in research/statistics/data/s7b-between.json. Tests with fixtures: tests/statistics-s7b-between.test.mjs. One of them changes the future bars and checks that no earlier reading moves.

What could be wrong

  • Survivors only: these are today's names. Companies that fell apart are missing, which flatters “buy the low”.
  • Bunching on the same dates: this matters most for the flagged rules (see above). The 95% ranges are too narrow.
  • Many tries: 254 rules were tried. The split into two halves is the guard against luck, and every top rule slipped in the second half.
  • Report dates: a report missing from the export merges two stretches into one. Stretches over 90 sessions are dropped for that reason. A report with no time is treated as after the close.
  • The parallel channel is a port of your Pine script onto daily bars, counted in sessions rather than clock time. It is not a pixel copy of TradingView's drawing.
  • “The lowest close” and the swing points use later bars by design. The rules never do: they enter at the first close where the condition is visible.

What I did not do

  • No push, no deploy, no database write, no paid model call. The bars were not fetched again.
  • No costs, slippage, stops or position sizing. No test of rules within a single name — everything is pooled across names.
  • S7's channel code was not changed; I used it as it is. The straight-line channel is added alongside it, not in place of it.
