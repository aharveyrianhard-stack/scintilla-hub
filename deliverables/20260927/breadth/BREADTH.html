WHOLE-MARKET BREADTH

Lane B1 · 27 Sep 2026 · every US common stock, and the S&P 500 — not the Hub's own list.

What this page shows

How many stocks are taking part in a move. The index can rise because a handful of giants rise while most stocks fall. These numbers count the stocks themselves, so they show that split.

Until now every breadth number on the Hub was measured on our own 364 names (bond funds included). As Alan put it on 11 Aug, "my Hub is not the market." This lane builds the market's own numbers: every US common stock (about 4,000–5,000 names on a given day), and separately the S&P 500 members of that day, back to September 2003.

The advance / decline line · US common stocks · since 2003

Each day, the number of stocks that closed higher minus the number that closed lower, added onto a running total. The height of the line means nothing; its direction does. When the S&P 500 makes a new high and this line does not, fewer stocks are carrying the index. Green = the line rose that day, red = it fell.

What each number means, and where it comes from

numberin plain wordshow it is counted
Advancing / decliningHow many stocks closed up, and how many closed down.Today's close against the previous session's close, after adjusting for splits. A stock that did not trade the day before is not counted.
Up volume / down volumeHow many shares traded in the rising stocks, and in the falling ones.Today's share volume, summed on each side.
A/D lineThe running total of (up − down) since September 2003.Adds one day at a time; the nightly run carries on from the last stored day.
TRIN (Arms index)Whether volume is flowing into the risers or the fallers. Below 1 leans toward the risers; above 1, toward the fallers.(up ÷ down) ÷ (up volume ÷ down volume). Left blank on a day where one side is empty.
% above the 50-day / 200-dayThe share of stocks trading above their own 50-day or 200-day average.Close above the simple average of its last 50 (or 200) closes, today included. A stock with less history than that is left out, and the row says how many were counted.
52-week highs / lowsHow many stocks hit a new one-year high, and how many a new one-year low.Today's high above the highest high of the previous 252 sessions (and the same for lows). Only stocks with a full year of history count.
Members countedHow many stocks went into the counts that day.US common stock (Massive's reference list, type "CS": no ETFs, funds, warrants or preferreds) with a bar today and a bar the session before. For the S&P 500: the members on that day.
  • Prices: Massive's daily bars for every US stock.
    • 2003-09-10 → 2026-08-17: the day files already in our R2 archive (5,770 sessions).
    • From 2026-08-18: Massive's "grouped daily" call, one call per session, which returns every stock at once.
    Both are raw prices. We proved this on CRWD for 18 Jun 2026: the day file equals adjusted=false, and both are 4× the adjusted price. So the job adjusts for splits itself, using Massive's split list, before it computes any average.
  • S&P 500 membership: FMP's current member list plus FMP's list of changes, rolled back so each day uses the members of that day. If the batch app has no FMP key, the S&P 500 rows are skipped and the status says so. They are never filled with today's list presented as history.
  • Where it lives:
    • The job writes to R2 (normalized/breadth_daily_v1/).
    • The chart API serves it at /v1/breadth, with freshness at /v1/breadth/status.
    • The edge function breadth-ingest copies it into public.breadth_daily every hour, which the Sentiment room and /allocation read.
  • Nightly: A Fly machine starts every hour and does nothing unless it is after 16:45 ET on a trading day and a session is missing. It then fetches one grouped call plus that day's splits and appends one row per scope.
    • If a pass fails, the next hour retries it.
    • A second pass in the same evening finds nothing missing and writes nothing.
    • A test proves the nightly append gives exactly the same rows as rebuilding the whole history.

Cross-check 1 · against public.market_internals (FMP)

market_internals holds 16 whole-market rows from FMP, 10 Aug → 24 Sep. They cover every NYSE, NASDAQ and AMEX listing (about 23,000, ETFs and funds included), so they will never equal our common-stock counts. The check is whether the two agree on direction and roughly on proportion.

Something we found: the date on those rows is the day the job ran, about 20:00 ET, not the session. Four rows are dated on a weekend; each actually describes the Friday before. Two rows (13 Aug and 3 Sep) have about half the usual volume, which looks like a partial read. The cross-check maps each row to its real session:

What could be wrong

  • A missed split looks like a stock falling 75% (or jumping 4× on a reverse split). The job counts any name that moved more than 50% in a day (suspect_moves), so a spike in that count on a date points straight at a missing split.
  • What counts as common stock comes from Massive's reference list today, including delisted names. A ticker that was once an ETF and later reused by a company could be counted on the wrong side. ADRs are left out on purpose.
  • S&P 500 history depends on FMP's change list. A company that changed its ticker (FB → META) may be missed on the old ticker for its earlier years. The members column shows it: a day far from ~500 means membership gaps.
  • Tiny stocks count the same as giants, as in every breadth measure. No price or volume floor is applied.
  • The 27 sessions from 19 Aug to 25 Sep come from grouped calls rather than the archive. The data is the same, but the source is different, and the status file records which days came from where.
  • The grouped call for tonight may not be finished at 16:45 ET. An answer with fewer than 5,000 stocks is treated as not ready: nothing is written, and the next hour tries again.
