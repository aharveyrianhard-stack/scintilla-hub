It is changing where money sits when there is no trade on: in an index core, not in cash.

My read, and the two that fit best

Is a long portfolio plus a short-term conviction portfolio how I would set it up? Yes — with two changes to how you put it.

  1. The long portfolio is not a second portfolio. It is where all money lives when it is not in a conviction trade. It needs no buy signal of its own. In the test, sending a sold conviction position back to the index instead of to cash was worth +1.8 points a year over 21 years and +6.7 points over the last two — with the very same picks.
  2. Cash needs one written reason, and it should be a slow one. The ladder asks the market to prove it is cheap before it lets money in; HEAT1 found its washed-out reading on about one day in twenty. A trend rule asks the market to prove it is broken before it takes money out. Checked on three closes in a row it was in cash 18% of the last 21 years and cut the worst fall from 55.2% to 21.9%. Checked every single day it whipsawed: 92 exits, 62 of which cost money.

What the six tests say, shortest first: time in the market did most of the work; a fast exit rule is worse than a slow one; waiting for a nearby level is cheap (a resting order at the 50-day average ended a year later 0.43 points behind buying at once on average, and the level came within six months in all but 0.4% of starts) while waiting for a rare one is expensive; and selling options pays less than it looks — Cboe's real put-selling index made +7.3% a year against SPY's +11.0% and still fell 37.1% at its worst.

Best fit 1 · Core + satellite

Fundamentals pick what: the satellite slots are where the knockout's kept names go. Nothing else on the list has a seat for a fundamentals pick.

Technicals pick when: a pick is bought at a level and sold on a close under a line. The line decides the sale, not a view on the market.

Stop missing legs: a sold pick lands in the index the same day, so the money is never waiting for the next idea. That rule alone was worth +1.8 points a year.

Honest limit: with a mechanical stand-in for your picks (the strongest sector funds), the satellite itself did not beat the index — 38% of 357 positions did. The structure kept +10.1% a year of SPY's +11.0%. It keeps you invested; the picks have to earn the rest.

Best fit 2 · Trend-filtered core, the slow version

Technicals pick when, for the whole portfolio: the core is in the index unless SPY is under its 200-day average AND most sectors are under theirs, three closes running. That is the only door to cash.

Stop missing legs: it was invested 82% of the last 21 years and 91% of the last two, against the ladder's 41%.

What it buys: the 2008 crash cost it 15.7% against SPY's 55.2%. Its worst fall in 21 years was 21.9%.

Honest limit: it sells after a fall has started and buys back after a rise has started. It trailed buy-and-hold by 1.8 points a year, and its last exit (14 Mar 2025 to 15 May 2025, 43 sessions) ended with SPY +5.2% from where it sold. Only 3 of its 30 exits saved money; those few were the big ones.

What I would not lead with. Dual momentum has the best numbers on the page, but most of that is QQQ, chosen today knowing it won; move its monthly look by a few days and the yearly return runs from +11.9% to +14.2%. What the public record says

  • Waiting in cash has usually cost money, though not always. Vanguard compared investing a sum at once with drip-feeding it over three months and with staying in cash. Investing at once won about two times in three; the gap is the return given up while part of the money sat idle. In the worst outcomes the order flips: the fully invested path ended lower. The study is about a new lump sum judged after one year; it does not test repeated in-and-out switching on technical signals. MSCI World index, rolling one-year windows, 1976-2022: investing at once beat a 3-month drip-feed 68% of the time and beat cash 70% of the time; the drip-feed beat cash 69% of the time. US shares beat cash (3-month Treasury bill) 76% of the time over 1976-2022. On $100,000 all in shares, the middle outcome after one year was $111,940 invested at once versus $109,580 drip-fed (2.2% more). Worst 5% of outcomes: $82,947 invested at once versus $85,906 drip-fed. When the waiting cash earns Treasury-bill interest, investing at once still won 65% of the time; the higher the cash rate, the smaller the advantage. Megan Finlay, Josef Zorn (Vanguard Research) 2023
  • Jumping between shares and cash only pays if the calls are right far more often than a coin flip. Sharpe's 1975 study, as summarised in a 2021 open-access re-test: with one switch a year and a 2% cost per switch over 1929-1972, a timer had to be right 83% of the time to beat simply holding shares, and 74% of the time to beat a fixed shares-and-cash mix of the same risk. That is where his rule of thumb 'at least 7 times out of 10' comes from. The 2021 re-test uses a cheaper 1% switching cost and data to 2018, and finds a lower bar for the average timer, but once luck in the order of mistakes is allowed for it ends up back near Sharpe's figure. Sharpe (1929-1972, 2% per switch): 83% accuracy needed to beat buy-and-hold [just holding shares]; 74% to beat a constant mix [a fixed blend of shares and cash]. 2021 re-test (1% per switch): average timer beats buy-and-hold at 71% or better in 1929-1972 and 61% or better in 1973-2018, and beats the constant mix at 63% or better in both periods. Simulation of 10,000 timers over 1929-2018: the middle timer is ahead of the constant mix from 63%; all of them are ahead only from 72%. Shares beat cash in 68.89% of calendar years 1929-2018; in the other years shares averaged -10.80% against +4.33% for cash. Simple yearly averages 1929-2018: US shares 10.92%, cash 4.18%; worst single year for shares -43.12%. Luigi Buzzacchi, Luca Ghezzi 2021
  • A tiny number of days decides long-run results, and the best days sit close to the worst ones. This is the classic case against stepping out of the market: an investor who leaves after a bad day tends to miss the rebound. Dow Jones index, 1900-2006, 29,190 trading days, dividends excluded: $100 grew to $25,746 (5.3% a year). Missing the best 10 days left $9,008 (65% less, 4.3% a year). Across 15 countries and 160,278 trading days, missing the best 10 days left portfolios 50.8% smaller on average. J.P. Morgan Guide to Retirement 2026 (primary document, S&P 500 with dividends, $10,000 from 2 Jan 2006 to 31 Dec 2025): 11.0% a year fully invested ($80,619); 6.6% without the 10 best days ($35,866); 3.8% without the 20 best ($21,177); 1.6% without the 30 best. Six of the 10 best days fell within two weeks of the 10 worst days. Javier Estrada 2008 · J.P. Morgan Asset Management 2026 · Brian Anderson (401(k) Specialist) 2024 · Thomas Barnet (MarketScreener) 2026
  • EVIDENCE AGAINST: the 'missing the best days' argument is one-sided. Missing the worst days helps by as much or more, and both kinds of extreme day cluster when the market is already falling. So a structure that sends sale money straight back into the index during a downtrend keeps it in the zone where the wild days live (that last step is reasoning, not a tested result). The three authors read the same pattern three ways: Estrada concludes timing is a losing game, Faber concludes avoiding falling markets is worthwhile, and Asness says the effect is symmetric so it proves nothing either way. Estrada: avoiding the worst 10 days on the Dow raised the end value by 206% (to $78,781); across 15 countries by 150.4% on average. Roughly 60-80% of the best and worst days came after the market had already started falling (below its 200-day average price); in the other countries studied, 76% of worst days and 67% of best days. Asness (AQR), S&P 500 monthly, Jan 1970-Dec 1996: 12.3% a year fully invested, 7.2% without the best 12 months, 18.1% without the worst 12 months. Javier Estrada 2008 · Mebane T. Faber 2011 · CXO Advisory Group 2011 · Steve Garmhausen (Financial Planning) 2011 · Cliff Asness (AQR) 2025 (written 1999)
  • Investors as a group earn less than the funds they own because of when they buy and sell. The gap was close to zero in broad US stock index funds and widest in the jumpiest funds. That supports a calm, broad US index core, but the 'index' label alone does not protect: outside US stock funds, index-fund investors had wider gaps than active-fund investors. A 2026 academic paper also disputes how much of the gap is really bad timing. Morningstar, nearly 23,000 US funds and ETFs, 10 years to 31 Dec 2025: the average dollar earned 8.7% a year versus 9.9% for the funds, a gap of 1.2 points a year (about 12% of the total return, roughly $3.8 trillion). US stock funds: 12.8% versus 13.3%. US stock index funds: 13.7% versus 13.8% (gap 0.1). Sector funds: gap 1.2 points (sector index funds 1.5, sector ETFs 1.8). International stock funds: index 1.6 versus active 0.8. Most volatile fifth of funds: gap 2.1 points; least volatile fifth: 0.4 (the fifths in between: 1.5, 1.8, 1.3, so not a smooth staircase). ETFs: gap 1.6 points versus 1.2 for ordinary funds. Counter-evidence: a Financial Analysts Journal paper (12 May 2026) using the sample from Morningstar's 2025 edition (2015-2024) puts the true cost of bad timing at 0.10% a year. Morningstar itself says the gap is not a parable of 'dumb money'. Jeffrey Ptak (Morningstar) 2026 · Jon A. Fulkerson, Bradford Jordan, Timothy Brand 2026

THE EXACT RULE TESTED
  • The core is SPY (the S&P 500 fund). Up to 30% of the portfolio can sit in a 'satellite': three slots of 10% each.
  • Alan's real conviction picks come from his fundamentals knockout, which has no stored history, so the test uses a mechanical stand-in: recent strength.
  • On the last trading day of each month, the candidates are ranked by their total return (price plus dividends) over the last 63 trading days — about three months.
  • A candidate qualifies for an empty slot if it is in the top 3, its price is above its own 50-day average, and it has beaten SPY over those three months.
  • A satellite already held is kept at month end while it is still in the top 5 and above its 50-day average.
  • Every day: a satellite that closes below its 50-day average is sold. Every order is filled at the next day's close.
  • Headline version: the money from a sold satellite goes straight back into SPY, so the portfolio is always fully invested (SPY = 100% minus 10% per satellite held).
  • Comparison version: the money from a sold satellite, and any empty slot, waits in cash (Treasury bills) until a month end fills the slot — the core stays at 70% SPY.
  • Every version starts on 3 January 2005 with 100% in SPY; the first satellites can be picked at the end of that month (and that is when the comparison version first moves down to its 70% core).
  • Candidates, honest version: the 11 sector funds plus the semiconductor fund SMH (a fund joins the list once it has a year of history).
  • Candidates, HINDSIGHT version: 13 stocks picked because we already know they became winners — shown for the mechanics only.
  • Positions are trimmed back to their targets only when something is sold, when the set of satellites changes at a month end, or when a holding has drifted more than 3 points from target at a month end.
  • The satellite picks here are a recent-strength rule, not Alan's fundamentals knockout — the knockout's picks have no history to test. So this shows how the STRUCTURE behaves, not how good the real picks would be.
  • The honest (sector-fund) version did not beat simply holding SPY: 10.11% a year against 10.96% over the full window (-0.85 points a year), and +36.5% against +39.6% over the last two years (-3.1 points). All 6 settings tried finished behind SPY over the full window (from -1.34 to -0.75 points a year), as did all 3 extra runs with a stricter buy test (from -1.08 to -0.71). Over the last two years alone 1 of the 9 runs finished ahead of SPY, by at most 0.4 points in total — far too little, over far too short a time, to mean anything. The differences between settings are small; picking the best one afterwards would itself be hindsight.
  • Why the honest version lagged: 357 satellite trades were finished, only 38% of them beat SPY over the same days (average +0.38% against SPY's +0.70%), and trading costs took about 0.31 points a year. The typical trade lasted 21 trading days and 18% lasted five days or fewer — a fund bought at month end can close below its 50-day average within days and be sold again.
  • The two 'today's known winners' versions use HINDSIGHT: the list of 13 stocks was chosen knowing how the story ended. Their returns could not have been earned and must not be set beside the honest versions as if they could.
  • With sold money going back into SPY the portfolio is 100% in stocks every day, so it falls about as far as the market in a crash (its worst fall was -54.1%, against SPY's -55.2%). This structure cures 'sitting in cash'; it does not cushion a bear market.
  • 'Back into SPY' beat 'wait in cash' by 1.80 points a year (full window). The reason: the slots tend to be empty just after the market has fallen, and those turned out to be good days to own SPY. On the 1,340 days that closed with all three slots empty, SPY sat on average 12% below its high, and its average gain on the following day — scaled up to a yearly rate — was about 30 points above cash (against about 4 on the days when all three slots were full). That is an average of separate single days, not a return anyone earned over a year. The cash version did fall less in the worst periods (7.8 points shallower at worst).
  • A sector fund is a slice of the S&P 500, so a 10% satellite in, say, technology on top of SPY is a tilt towards something already owned, not a new source of return.
  • The last-two-years figures rest on a few dozen satellite trades in one particular market (34 sector-fund trades were opened on or after 3 Oct 2024 and closed by 5 Oct 2026; the 3 already held on that date are not in that count); they describe what happened, not what to expect.
  • Costs are counted at 0.05% per fund order and 0.10% per stock order, orders fill at the next day's close, and taxes on the frequent sales are not counted.
  • Some candidates arrive late: the real-estate fund XLRE from late 2016 and the communications fund XLC from mid 2019; in the hindsight list Google's bars start in April 2014, and NBIS and CRWV only exist from 2024-25.
  • The numbers 63 days, 50 days, top 3 and 3 points are one reasonable choice — not tuned, and not proven best.

  • FOR: In Faber's back-test [a rule replayed on past prices, not real money] of the S&P 500 from 1901 to 2012, the once-a-month 10-month-average rule returned slightly more than buy-and-hold and cut the worst losses by roughly 40-50%. How our tools would feed it

  • The switch: R4's REGIME dial already holds this test (SPY against its 200-day, plus how many of our own served companies are above their 200-day and 50-day). This structure is its plainest form.
  • STRETCH stays out of it: Geiger percentiles, RSI / Williams and the VIX reading pace adds and trims inside the invested part. Under this structure they do not move money to cash.
  • Reviewed lines: SPY's and QQQ's 2W P1 are the early warning. A lost line with breadth already weak is the day to look, not yet the day to act.
  • Breadth: the test used sector funds so it could run 21 years. Over the last two years our own served-company breadth gave a similar answer (the last row of the table).

What could be wrong

  • The three-close wait was one of five versions set before the test, but it is still the best of five. Expect less from it than the table shows.
  • Trend rules have had a poor run since 2009 because falls have been short and sharp. April 2025 is the example: out near the low, back in +16.1% higher.
  • No taxes are counted. Every exit from a winning position would realise a gain.
THE EXACT RULE TESTED
  • The money sits in SPY (the S&P 500 fund) by default. It moves to cash (Treasury bills) only when a simple regime rule says the market's trend has broken, and moves back when the rule clears.
  • Trend = SPY's closing price against the average of its last 200 closes (the 200-day average). Under it = trend down.
  • Breadth [how many parts of the market are healthy] = the share of the sector funds (technology, health care, financials and so on) that closed above their own 200-day average. Under half = breadth weak.
  • Headline rule: go to cash only when SPY is under its 200-day average AND breadth is weak, both at once. Otherwise stay in SPY.
  • The rule is read at each close and acted on at the next session's close. Each switch costs 0.05% of the amount moved.
  • Four other ways of writing it are shown beside it: trend alone; a once-a-month check; the headline rule with a 3-day wait; and the headline rule using the Hub's own company breadth (last two years only).
  • The rule did not make more money than simply holding SPY: 7.53% a year against 10.96% over the whole history. What it bought was a shallower worst fall (-24.9% against -55.2%), and the big saving was one event, 2008. Measured from January 2010 it made 8.67% a year against 14.19% for holding, with a worst fall of -22.0% against -33.7%.
  • The rule is often wrong in a small way. Of the headline rule's 92 completed exits, 30 were followed by SPY being lower at the buy-back (the exit saved money) and 62 by SPY being higher (it cost money). The worst single one missed a 16.14% rise in SPY (sold 2025-04-04, bought back 2025-05-13).
  • Adding the breadth test to the trend test did not help in this history. It switched more often (92 exits against 72 for trend alone), made 7.53% a year against 8.13%, and its worst fall was -24.9% against -22.1%. When breadth hovers near half, the rule flips in and out.
  • Over the last two years the five rules made between 21.1% and 33.5%, against 39.6% for holding SPY. Of the 16 exits they made in that window, 13 were bought back at a higher price.
  • The 3-day wait has the best numbers of the four long-history rules, but it is being picked out after seeing the results, and 3 days is one untested choice. It was slower in the fast falls: -15.7% in the 2008 crash and -14.8% in the 2020 crash, against -13.6% and -10.5% for the headline rule.
  • A switch fills one session after the signal, at the close. In a fast fall much of the damage is done before the rule can act, and it usually buys back above where it sold.
  • Breadth here is 11 sector funds today but only 9 before 2016-07-25 (the real-estate fund is counted from then, the communications fund from 2019-04-04). It is a coarse reading of breadth, chosen because it has a long history with no hindsight.
  • The last-two-years variant uses the Hub's breadth, which is measured on TODAY'S list of served companies. Companies that failed or were dropped are not in it, so it flatters the past, and two years with a handful of signals proves nothing by itself.
  • The 200-day and 10-month lengths are the textbook settings, used as given. No other lengths were tried, so nothing here says the result holds for nearby settings.
  • No taxes are counted. In a taxable account every exit is a sale, which would lower these numbers.
  • One history, one market, one fund. A handful of large bear markets drive the result; that is a small sample.

  • Timing luck: the same rule read on a different day of the month made +11.9% to +14.2% a year, and +37.1% to +55.8% over the last two years.
  • Its exit is slow: it waits for a whole year's return to fall behind cash. It fell 27.9% in the 2020 crash and 18.1% in the 2022 bear market.
  • “Bonds” was not a safe place in 2022, when shares and bonds fell together.
  • One overseas fund's 2005 share split is unadjusted in our stored prices; the test corrects it in memory (see page specs).
THE EXACT RULE TESTED
  • Once a month, at the last close of the month, measure how much each fund made over the past year (252 trading days, dividends included).
  • Hold the one that made the most, with the whole account in that single fund (this is 'relative momentum': own the stronger one).
  • But if even the winner made no more than Treasury bills (cash) over that same year, hold 7-10 year Treasury bonds (the fund IEF) instead (this is 'absolute momentum': is it even beating cash?).
  • Any change is made at the next session's close. Nothing is looked at between month ends, however far the market moves.
  • The headline version chooses between SPY (the S&P 500) and QQQ (the Nasdaq 100). The published version chooses between SPY and EFA (developed markets outside the US). The sector version holds the top three of the eleven US sector funds, a third each. The blended version averages the 3-, 6- and 12-month returns instead of using 12 months alone.
  • The rule is never in cash: it is always in shares or in bonds. The starting holding on 3 Jan 2005 is what the rule read at the previous close (31 Dec 2004).
  • Every order pays 0.05% of the amount traded, so one full switch costs about 0.10% of the account. No taxes are counted.
  • Bonds here are IEF (7-10 year Treasuries). Antonacci's published rule uses a broad bond fund (AGG), but AGG has no dividend history in our tables, so its total return cannot be built. IEF moves more than a broad bond fund when interest rates move, in both directions.
  • The published rule compares the US with ALL markets outside the US; our overseas fund is EFA, which covers developed markets only (no emerging markets).
  • EFA's 3-for-1 share split of 9 Jun 2005 is not adjusted in our stored prices. For this study the earlier EFA prices are divided by 3 in memory (no file changed). Without that fix the published version shows a 66% one-day loss that never happened (full-window growth 8.7% a year and worst fall -33.7% instead of 8.7% and -33.7%). Only the published version uses EFA.
  • Timing luck: the same headline rule read on a different day of the month gives 11.94% to 13.75% a year over the full window, a worst fall of -30.9% to -28.6%, and 37.1% to 53.5% over the last two years. The month-end figure is one draw from that range, not the answer. Tried on all 21 possible days, the range is 11.61% to 14.34% a year (middle 13.24%), and the month-end run (14.18%) beats 20 of the 21: the headline number sits at the lucky end of the range, about a point a year above the middle.
  • The last two years hold very few decisions: 4 switches for the headline rule, 6 for the published one. Two years cannot show whether the rule works; the full window (32 switches in almost 22 years) is the better guide and even that is a small number.
  • The whole account sits in ONE fund (three in the sector version). When it is in shares it takes the full fall of that fund until the next month end; the rule only steps aside after a year-long return has dropped below cash, which is slow. Fast falls (2020) are over before it reacts.
  • 'Bonds' is not 'safe': in 2022 shares and bonds fell together. The headline rule moved to bonds in May 2022 and stayed a year; the bond fund lost money over that stay (see bond_spells), and the year 2022 still ended -16.4%.
  • SPY and QQQ were chosen today, knowing QQQ was the big winner of these 22 years; the headline rule sat in QQQ 66.4% of the time. Simply holding QQQ made 15.42% a year (worst fall -53.4%) against the rule's 14.18% (worst fall -28.6%): the rule did not add return over QQQ, it cut the worst fall. The published version, which has no QQQ, made 8.7% a year — less than holding SPY (10.96%).
  • 252 trading days stands in for Antonacci's 12 calendar months; the two differ by a day or two. The sector funds XLRE (from Oct 2015) and XLC (from Jun 2018) join the ranking only once they have 252 sessions of history, so the sector list is 9 funds until late 2016 and 11 only from mid 2019.
  • No taxes: every switch out of a winning fund would realise a gain in a taxable account.

What the public record says

  • The best-known case FOR it: cutting exposure to the US stock market after volatile months and raising it after calm ones gave a better return per unit of risk over 1926-2015. The test relied on leverage [borrowed money] in calm periods, and the authors set its scaling constant using the whole 90-year sample. US stock market, monthly, 1926-2015 (NBER working paper 22208, April 2016, revised June 2016): alpha [return not explained by simply holding the market] 4.86% a year (standard error 1.56), beta 0.61; Sharpe ratio [return per unit of bumpiness] 0.42 buy-and-hold vs 0.52 managed; $1 grew to about $20,000 vs about $4,000 for buy-and-hold at the same overall volatility (nominal, log-scale chart). Exposure used: median 0.93x, 75th percentile 1.59x, 90th 2.64x, 99th 6.39x. Break-even trading cost 56 basis points [0.56%]. By 30-year thirds the alpha was 8.11% (1926-1955), 2.06% with standard error 2.82, i.e. not distinguishable from zero (1956-1985), and 4.22% (1986-2015). Alan Moreira, Tyler Muir 2016 (working paper, revised June 2016), 2017 (journal)
  • RETURN versus SMOOTHNESS, from the same paper: remove the borrowing and the rule still looks good on a risk-adjusted basis, but its raw return drops sharply, because all it can then do is step down toward cash. The authors write that after the autumn-2008 falls their strategy 'cashed out almost completely and returned to the market only as the spike in volatility receded'. Same data, 1926-2015. Capped at 100% invested (no leverage): average excess return [return above T-bills] 5.61% a year vs 9.47% uncapped; alpha 2.12% vs 4.86%; Sharpe ratio unchanged at 0.52; exposure median 0.93x and at the 1.0x cap from the 75th percentile up. Capped at 1.5x (the paper's 'standard margin requirement'): 7.18% a year, alpha 3.10%, Sharpe 0.53. Derived by the fact-checker, not printed in the paper: alpha 4.86 and beta 0.61 with a 9.47% managed return imply buy-and-hold earned about 7.6% a year, so the no-leverage version (5.61%) earned LESS raw return than simply holding the market, at lower volatility. Alan Moreira, Tyler Muir 2016 (working paper, revised June 2016), 2017 (journal)
  • A wider study by Duke and Man Group researchers found the return-per-risk gain only in stocks and credit. Its clearest effect across assets was steadier volatility and fewer extreme months in BOTH directions; worst months were milder for 'almost all' assets, but not for US bonds over 1963-2017, where they were slightly worse. Results are shown after the fact at a fixed 10% volatility, using a scaling constant chosen with hindsight. US equities 1927-2017, scaled and unscaled both set to 10% yearly volatility: Sharpe 0.40 unscaled vs 0.48-0.51 scaled; vol of vol [how much the bumpiness itself varies] 4.6% vs 1.8% (20-day half-life); average of the worst 1% of one-month returns -11.4% vs -9.0%; average of the best 1% +11.8% vs +7.3% (so 4.5 points were shaved off the best months to save 2.4 points on the worst); turnover 2.39 round-trips a year at a 20-day half-life; average exposure 52% unscaled vs 70% scaled to reach the same 10% volatility. Sharpe did not improve in the middle sub-period (text says 1957-1987, the data table says 1958-1987). US bonds 1963-2017: Sharpe fell from 0.25 to 0.04-0.09 net of costs (0.05-0.09 gross), and the worst-1% average went from -8.4% to between -8.6% and -9.5%. Balanced 60/40 portfolio (in risk terms, S&P 500 and 10-year Treasury futures) 1988-2017: Sharpe 0.80 unscaled, 0.87 with asset-level scaling, 0.91 gross / 0.90 net with asset- and portfolio-level scaling; worst-1% average -9.7% to -7.3%. Campbell R. Harvey, Edward Hoyle, Russell Korgao 2018
  • The strongest evidence AGAINST: across 103 stock strategies the volatility-managed version beat the plain version only about half the time, and a real-time investor who mixed the managed and plain versions using only past data usually ended up worse off than one who simply held the plain strategy. Momentum-type strategies were the main exception. 103 US equity strategies (the 9 factors from the original paper plus 94 anomaly portfolios): managed version had the higher Sharpe ratio in 53 cases, the plain version in 50; only 8 differences were statistically significant, concentrated in momentum-related strategies. In hindsight regressions 77 of 103 showed positive alpha (23 significantly positive, 3 significantly negative), but the real-time combination earned a lower certainty-equivalent return [the guaranteed return an investor would accept in place of the risky one] in 72 of 103 cases. For the stock market itself, real-time Sharpe 0.42 for the combination vs 0.46 for the plain market. Out of sample, managed momentum, profitability (ROE) and betting-against-beta still gave 'large utility gains'. Scott Cederburg, Michael S. O'Doherty, Feifei Wa 2020
  • Other critics traced much of the paper gain to hindsight and trading costs. The debate is not closed: a 2024 Journal of Finance paper argues a more elaborate version, timing nine long-short factors at once, does survive both objections. That version is an institutional construction, not something a long-only private account can copy. Liu, Tang, Zhou (2019): once look-ahead bias [using information not known at the time] is corrected, maximum drawdown [worst peak-to-trough fall] is 68%-93% 'in almost all cases' and the strategy 'outperforms the market only during the financial crisis period'. Barroso and Detzel (2021): after transaction costs, volatility management of factors besides the market 'generally produces zero abnormal returns and significantly reduces Sharpe ratios'; the managed market portfolio is robust to costs but 'only provides superior performance when sentiment is high'. DeMiguel, Martin-Utrera, Uppal (2024): conditional multifactor portfolio has an out-of-sample, net-of-costs Sharpe ratio 13% higher than the unconditional one (significant at the 1% level); traded one at a time, 4 of the 9 managed factors had a negative net-of-cost average return. Fang Liu, Xiaoxiao Tang, Guofu Zhou 2019 · Pedro Barroso, Andrew Detzel 2021 · Victor DeMiguel, Alberto Martin-Utrera, Raman Up 2024

THE EXACT RULE TESTED
  • PART A, spreading the money. Once a month, at the last close of the month, measure how jumpy each holding has been over its last 63 trading days, about three months [jumpiness = volatility: the typical size of its daily moves, dividends included].
  • Equal risk: give each holding money in proportion to 1 ÷ its jumpiness, so a calm holding gets more money and a jumpy one less, and each is expected to swing the portfolio by about the same amount. The shares are scaled to add up to 100%.
  • Equal money: the plain comparison, the same amount in every holding [1/N], reset at the same month ends.
  • Basket one is the sector funds: 9 of them in 2005, 11 today. A fund joins at the first month end after it has 252 sessions (a year) of history (XLRE from 2016-10-31, XLC from 2019-06-28).
  • Basket two is today's leading stocks: 5 of them in 2005, 13 today. A stock joins at the first month end after it has 126 sessions (six months) of history (AVGO from 2010-02-26, TSLA from 2010-12-31, META from 2012-11-30, GOOGL from 2014-10-31, VST from 2017-11-30, BE from 2019-01-31, NBIS from 2025-04-30, CRWV from 2025-09-30). This basket was picked with hindsight.
  • Part A is always 100% invested: it changes how the money is spread, never how much is in the market.
  • PART B, a steady risk level. Every day at the close, measure SPY's jumpiness over its last 20 trading days and express it as a yearly figure. SPY share = 15% ÷ that figure (a second version uses 12%), never more than 100%; the rest sits in Treasury bills. Example: SPY running at 30% a year gives a 50% share; at 15% or calmer it is fully invested.
  • To avoid trading every day, an order is sent only when the wanted share is 10 points or more away from the share actually held.
  • Every decision uses only what was known at that close and is filled at the next session's close, paying the study's standard trading cost.
  • The leading-stocks basket is hindsight: these are today's winners, so its returns say nothing about what a stock picker could have earned.
  • Even the comparison between the two weightings of that basket is tilted. In a basket chosen because every member won, the jumpiest members were the biggest winners, so giving them less money (equal risk: 30.95% a year against 35.52% for equal money) is bound to look worse than it would in a basket chosen without hindsight. The part that does carry over is the smoother ride: yearly jumpiness 27.1% against 30.0%.
  • The leading-stocks basket is not the same thing all the way through: it starts with 5 stocks and ends with 13. Our price history for GOOGL starts on 2014-04-03 and for NBIS on 2024-10-21, although Google has been listed since 2004 and Nebius's predecessor company traded for years before, so both are missing from the basket's early years.
  • In its early years the leading-stocks basket held only 5 stocks, and equal risk put as much as 49.6% of the money in one of them (MSFT, 2005-02-28). That is concentration, not spreading.
  • Part A never holds cash, so it does not protect in a crash: in the 2008 crash the equal-risk sector basket lost 49.8% against 53.3% for equal money and 55.2% for SPY, and that was also its deepest fall of the whole test.
  • Both sector baskets trailed plain SPY: 9.89% (equal risk) and 10.17% (equal money) a year against 10.96% over the full window, and 18.9% and 23.5% against 39.6% over the last two years, because spreading across sectors holds far less technology than SPY does.
  • Equal risk here looks at each holding on its own. It ignores that the holdings rise and fall together, so it does not make every holding contribute the same share of the portfolio's total risk.
  • The jumpiness measure looks backward (63 sessions for Part A, 20 for Part B). It only rises after prices have already started to swing, so the cut comes after the first part of a fall, and the return to full size comes after the first part of the rebound. In the 2025-02-19 to 2025-04-08 fall the 15% rule first sold with SPY already 9.3% down, was still selling after the low (its smallest share, 29.4%, came 8 sessions after it) because violent rebound days count as jumpiness too, and was not back to 90% until SPY had already risen 19.4% from the low. Its round trip (peak, low, 40 sessions on) was -8.4% against -3.0% for SPY.
  • Part B does not deliver its label: with no borrowing the share is capped at 100%, so in calm markets it is simply SPY. The 15% version's own yearly jumpiness over the full window came out at 13.0%, the 12% version's at 11.6%.
  • The 10-point no-trade band means the held share can sit up to 10 points away from the wanted share for long stretches: over the full window the 15% formula wanted 100% on 63.2% of sessions but the portfolio was at 100% on 36.3%. That idle sliver of cash is part of the result: letting the 15% rule always step up to 100% when the formula says so would have made 10.08% a year instead of 9.97%, and 28.7% instead of 27.9% over the last two years.
  • The 15% and 12% targets, the 20- and 63-session windows and the 10-point band are the brief's round numbers. Nothing was tuned, but other choices would give different results, and a single 22-year history has only three big falls to learn from. In the last two years the 15% rule sold on the way down in only 1 of the 3 SPY pullbacks of 5% or more, so the recent scorecard rests on very few events.
  • The sector funds changed shape during the test: real estate was carved out of financials in 2016 and communications was built partly from technology and consumer discretionary in 2018, so the basket of 2005 is not the basket of today.
  • A data blemish: XLF's price series falls 0.7% on 2016-09-19, the day the real-estate fund was spun out of it, while SPY moved 0.0%. That looks like a spin-off the source prices only partly adjust for. It takes about 0.06% off the equal-risk sector basket and 0.08% off the equal-money one, once; too small to change any conclusion.
  • No taxes are counted. Monthly resets and the Part B orders would create taxable sales in a normal account.

  • The limit-order problem is real and documented, but the research is not one-sided. A buy order resting below the market fills when the price falls to it, which can be because of bad news, and does not fill when good news sends the price away. Handa and Schwartz name exactly these two risks. Linnainmaa, using every individual investor's trades in Finland, found that much of individuals' apparent bad timing (poor returns after trading, losses around earnings, buying what is falling) arises mechanically because limit orders suffer from adverse selection (they tend to be filled by people who know more). That favourable evidence is about orders at or near today's price, not orders parked well below it. No single percentage in the abstracts. How our tools would feed it

  • The levels: the 21-day and 50-day averages need no review and can be tested. Reviewed lines are better levels but cannot be tested honestly: they were drawn this autumn with all of history in view.
  • A ladder from the lines (a proposal, no file defines it yet): group a name's lines that sit within 1% into one shelf; tranche one, two and three are the first three shelves under price that price has already closed above; a daily close under the third cancels the plan.
  • Pace: the Geiger says how hot the name already is; SG1's “cold for itself” says whether a low reading is low for that name. Lines say where, these say whether.
  • Fundamentals overrule: a broken growth story cancels the plan whatever the chart says.

What could be wrong

  • These are index funds. A single stock can run from a level and not return for a year; the deadline matters far more there, and that was not tested.
  • On the comparison table this structure's row is the trend rule re-entered in thirds (+6.9% a year) against re-entered at once (+7.5%). After a real break the market tends to run, so thirds bought back less.
  • Ten breakouts in two years is too few to judge buying the retest of a broken line.
THE EXACT RULE TESTED
  • The question: when you are in cash and want to own a fund, is it better to buy it all today, to buy it in three parts by the calendar, or to buy it in three parts as the price comes back down to its average lines?
  • The two lines are the 21-day average (an exponential average, which leans on the most recent days — roughly the last month of trading) and the 50-day average (a simple average of the last 50 closes — roughly the last ten weeks).
  • The entry experiment: on the first trading day of every month since January 2005, for SPY, QQQ and SMH, 1.00 of cash is put to work five ways and valued about six months (126 sessions) and about a year (252 sessions) later. Waiting cash earns the Treasury-bill rate.
  • All at once: buy everything at day one's close. Calendar thirds: a third on day one, a third 21 sessions later, a third 42 sessions later.
  • Level thirds: a third on day one; one buy order left resting at the 21-day average and one at the 50-day average. Each order is set from the previous close's average, so its price is known before the day opens; it fills if that day's low reaches it (at the open if the price gaps under it). If the price is already at or under a line on day one, that third is bought on day one. A third that never fills stays in bills — it never chases.
  • Level thirds with a deadline: the same, but any third still not bought 63 sessions (about three months) after day one is bought at that day's close.
  • Wait for the pullback with everything: all the money sits on one resting order at the 50-day average, with no deadline (the cash-by-default habit). If the price is already at or under that line on day one, everything is bought that day.
  • The breakout retest check: for the ten breakouts of the last two years (first daily close above a confirmed 2-week pivot high), did the low come back to within 0.5% of the broken line in the next 20 sessions, and did buying half at the breakout and half on a resting order just above the line beat buying it all at the breakout?
  • The two continuous runs for the comparison table use one simple in-or-out rule on SPY: OUT (all in Treasury bills) only when SPY closes under its 200-day average AND fewer than half of the sector funds are above their own 200-day average; IN otherwise. One run buys back everything when the rule turns IN; the other buys back in thirds at the same two lines, with the 63-session deadline, checked on closing prices.
  • In the two continuous runs every decision is taken at a close and traded at the next session's close, and each order costs 0.05% of the amount traded.
  • The monthly start dates overlap heavily: a six-month result shares five of its six months with the next start's result. About 260 starts per fund are far fewer than 260 independent tests.
  • 2005 to 2026 was, on balance, a strongly rising market for all three funds. In a rising market any method that waits tends to lose to buying at once, simply because it spends less time invested. A long falling market would favour the waiting methods.
  • The average lines move. In a rising market the 50-day average climbs while you wait, so an order that fills 'on the pullback' often fills at a higher price than day one's close (the resting_order_behaviour table shows how often).
  • A resting order is counted as filled whenever the day's low touched its price. In real trading an order that is only just touched can go unfilled, so the level methods are flattered slightly.
  • The entry experiment and the breakout check charge no trading costs (the cost would be at most 0.05% of the money, nearly the same for every method). The two continuous runs do charge costs.
  • The last-two-years table has few start dates, all in a market that mostly rose, and they overlap; read it as an illustration, not as proof.
  • The breakout check is only 10 events in two funds over two years. In 9 of the 10, the breakout close was itself already inside the 0.5% band above the line, so 'coming back to within 0.5%' mostly means the price had not yet left (8 of the retests are on the very next session) — it is not evidence that breakouts usually pull back. The extra columns for a return to the line itself are the stricter test: that happened in 6 of the 9 breakouts that have 20 full sessions behind them.
  • The breakout of QQQ on 2026-10-02 has only 1 session(s) after it in the data; it is shown but left out of the averages.
  • In the two continuous runs the levels are checked on closing prices and traded a session later, so they are not the same thing as the resting intraday orders of the entry experiment.
  • The in-or-out rule changes its mind often around the 200-day average: it turned IN 92 times, and 54 of those were sold again within 5 sessions. In 45 of the 92 the thirds version was sold again with only one third bought. So its smaller falls owe a lot to simply holding less during those flips, which is a different thing from 'buying at better prices'.
  • When the rule turns IN, SPY is often still under its 21-day and 50-day averages, so the second and last thirds are bought within a day or two; in a sharp V-shaped recovery the opposite happens and the thirds version sits two-thirds in cash for weeks while the market runs (May to August 2025 is the example in the last two years).

6 · Getting paid to wait

In plain words: you sell someone the right to make you buy SPY at a price below today's (a cash-secured put — the cash to buy is set aside). You are paid a premium now. If SPY is above that price at the end of the month you keep the premium and repeat. If it is below, you buy at your price, however far it has fallen. A covered call is the mirror: you are paid to promise to sell shares you hold at a higher price. The measured record is sobering: Cboe's put-selling index made +7.3% a year since 2007 against SPY's +11.0%, and still fell 37.1% at its worst. Its covered-call index made +6.3% a year since 2005. You are paid to wait, and you give up most of every strong rally for it.

THE EXACT RULE TESTED
  • A put you SELL is a promise to buy SPY at a set price [the strike] on a set date; you are paid a fee [the premium] up front for making it, and the full purchase price waits in Treasury bills meanwhile [cash-secured].
  • A call you SELL on shares you own is a promise to sell them at a set price on a set date; you are paid a premium, and give up any rise beyond that price [covered call].
  • Every 21 sessions (about a month) the rule sells a fresh 21-session option at the close and the old one settles on that same close. Nothing is predicted: the calendar alone decides the day.
  • Put at the money: always in cash, promising to buy at today's close. If SPY ends lower, the shortfall is paid from the cash; SPY is never owned (the Cboe PutWrite index works this way).
  • Put 2% below: the same with the promise set 2% under today's close - a smaller premium, and the first 2% of a fall costs nothing.
  • Covered call 2% above: hold SPY all the time and promise to sell 2% over today's close; a month's rise beyond 2% is paid away in cash and the shares are kept.
  • The wheel: sell puts 2% below while in cash; if SPY ends under the strike you buy there. While holding, sell calls 2% above; if SPY ends over the strike the shares go at that price and you are back in cash selling puts.
  • We hold no option prices, so every premium is MODELLED, not measured: the standard option formula [Black-Scholes] fed with the market's own fear gauge [the VIX] at that close, then cut by 5% for the dealing spread and commission.
  • The account is revalued with the same model at every close, so the falls shown are daily, not just month-end.
  • Every premium here is modelled, not measured: we store no option prices. Real quotes differ from the formula, most of all in a panic, when the gap between the buying and selling price of an option widens far beyond the flat 5% assumed here.
  • The model pays the seller too much where it matters most. The VIX leans heavily on the prices of puts far below the market, so options AT the market usually trade below it, and calls above the market lower still. Fed the VIX as it stands, the at-the-money put and the covered call come out better than the real thing. Where Cboe publishes a measured index for the same strategy (PUT, BXY), believe the index, not this model.
  • How much of the result is the model: the at-the-money put makes 10.6% a year with the VIX fed in whole, 7.9% at nine-tenths of it and 5.3% at eight-tenths. The covered call makes 13.5% a year as published (SPY itself: 11.0%), 10.9% with the call input at 0.80 x VIX and 8.4% at 0.70. The modelled covered call beating plain SPY is the model talking, not a finding.
  • For puts well BELOW the market the lean runs the other way: they usually cost more than the VIX implies [the skew]. For a put only 2% below we cannot tell from our own data which side the real price sits on; the re-runs bracket it, from 5.2% a year at 0.90 x VIX to 11.4% at 1.15 x VIX - the input decides the answer.
  • The model ignores SPY's dividends. That under-prices the puts but over-prices the calls, so the covered-call and wheel figures are flattered a little more; the dividend re-run in the extras puts a number on it.
  • The 21-session calendar starting 3 January 2005 is not the exchange's real expiry calendar (the third Friday), and the result depends on which day the cycle happens to land: the roll-day re-run shows the spread across all 21 possible calendars (1.1 to 2.5 points a year between the luckiest and the unluckiest, depending on the variant).
  • One SPY contract covers 100 shares, so these rules come in lumps of roughly 77,000 dollars of cash per put at today's price; the test assumes any fraction can be traded.
  • Real SPY options can be exercised early [American style], chiefly calls just before a dividend; the test treats them as settle-at-expiry only [European style]. Taxes are ignored: option premiums are taxed as short-term gains.
  • A sold put does not avoid a crash: it takes nearly the full fall beyond the strike, and in return gives up the rise. The stock share shown is the option's equivalent holding [delta] at each close, which climbs toward 100% exactly when SPY is falling. Premium collected is not profit - the settlements come out of it.
  • In the entry experiment the put strikes sit 2% to 10% below the market; for the deeper ones real puts are priced above the VIX, so the modelled premium there is, if anything, on the low side.
  • The entry experiment's last-two-years table has few starts and a half-year horizon, in a market that mostly rose: it shows what happened, not what to expect.

Alternatives you did not name

You asked for more than your own idea handed back. Three alternatives came out of the tests themselves, and the rest come from the public record (each line below survived an independent fact-check).

  • A floor under the ladder. Keep everything you have built; let the ladder steer only the part above a fixed index floor. Tested above: +32.6% / +34.0% / +35.4% with a 50 / 60 / 70% floor, against +25.6% as it was.

  • STOCK-REPLACEMENT BARBELL: keep most of the money in Treasury bills and spend a small slice on long-dated call options (LEAPS [options lasting up to about three years]), so the most you can lose is the option cost while the upside stays open. FOR: the loss is capped at the premium paid and the cash keeps earning interest; call-option portfolio strategies have been studied since the 1970s. AGAINST: by a textbook identity (put-call parity, stated from general knowledge) it is close to the same bet as owning the shares plus a protective put, so the evidence against buying protection applies - AQR on buying puts: 'the price of admission is generally higher than the benefit'; a model-based comparison found the floor formula (CPPI) superior to the bond-plus-option version in every case examined; the option can expire worthless after a flat or falling stretch and the premium must be paid again; calls pay no dividends. We found no published track record for this barbell as a private investor would run it. TESTABLE ON DAILY BARS: no - it needs historical option prices; a model-based estimate from share prices would understate the real cost. Options Industry Council: a LEAPS call gives the right to buy 'up to three years in the future'; the buyer's 'risk is limited to the price paid'; option investors 'run the risk of losing their entire investment in a relatively short period'. AQR 'Rebuffed' (99 option-based funds - the 99 of 624 in three Morningstar categories with records back to January 2020 - measured 1 Jan 2020-31 Jan 2025): more than two-thirds delivered lower returns with more risk than a passive mix of shares and T-bills, 81% had worse drawdowns than that mix, and only 14% beat it. Pezier & Scheller: CPPI superior to option-based portfolio insurance 'in all cases'. No verified return or drawdown figure for the barbell itself.   • TREND-FOLLOWING SLEEVE (managed futures [funds that go long rising markets and short falling ones across shares, bonds, commodities and currencies]) held in place of idle cash. FOR: Hurst, Ooi & Pedersen rebuild the strategy across 67 markets back to 1880 and find it profitable in each decade, little related to shares and bonds, and positive in 8 of the 10 worst falls of a 60/40 portfolio; the live index of the ten largest trend funds made money in three of the four big S&P 500 falls since 2000. AGAINST: it is not a share substitute - its link to shares is close to zero, so it cannot be relied on to capture a share rally and AQR itself says it 'may have lagged in bull markets'; it has its own drawdowns; it had a weak stretch in the 2010s that AQR puts down to fewer large market moves; it does little in very fast falls; results differ between managers; the century-long record is a simulation with assumed costs, written by a firm that sells the strategy; and an independent study finds the statistical evidence for the underlying effect weak.   • BUFFER (DEFINED-OUTCOME) ETFs: a fund that packages an options collar so that, if held for the full outcome period, the first slice of loss is absorbed and gains are capped.   • EQUAL-WEIGHT / BREADTH CATCH-UP SLEEVE: hold every share in an index in equal amounts instead of by company size, as a bet that the many catch up with the few giants.   • RISK-BUDGET FRAMING (volatility targeting [holding more when markets are calm and less when they are wild]): decide how much risk the portfolio should carry, then let each position's size follow from its recent volatility - so the question is 'how much', and the answer is never zero. FOR: Moreira & Muir find portfolios that take less risk when volatility is high produced large risk-adjusted gains; Harvey and co-authors, using more than 60 assets and daily data from 1926, find it raised Sharpe ratios for shares and credit, cut the chance of extreme returns and lowered maximum drawdowns for balanced portfolios. AGAINST: Cederburg and co-authors test 103 share strategies and find volatility-managed versions 'do not systematically outperform', and versions an investor could actually have run in real time generally did worse than leaving the portfolio alone; the published gains come partly from borrowing to hold MORE in calm markets, which a cash account cannot do; and because volatility usually rises when prices fall, the rule cuts exposure after a fall (Harvey et al. say this directly), which can leave you light in a sharp rebound (the rebound point is our inference) - a real drawback for someone tired of missing moves. TESTABLE ON DAILY BARS: yes, fully - recent volatility is computed straight from daily closing prices.   • The index evidence does not carry over automatically to a hand-picked share list. Bessembinder (2018): the majority of US common stocks since 1926 had lifetime returns below one-month Treasury bills, and the best 4% of companies explain the entire net gain of the market. 'Invested beats cash 70% of the time' is a statement about owning the whole market; for a concentrated list of individual shares the odds per share are worse, which is why the test on Scintilla's own picks matters. source
  • The headline default effect shrinks a lot over time. Choi, Laibson, Cammarota, Lombardo & Beshears (NBER, 2024, 'Smaller than We Thought?'): once job changes, withdrawals and later opt-outs are counted, automatic enrolment raises steady-state saving by only 0.6% of income and auto-escalation by 0.3%; only 40% of people let the first scheduled increase happen. The 37%-to-86% jump the researcher leads with is a first-year participation figure, not a long-run outcome. source
  • A default helps or hurts depending on what it is set to. Choi, Laibson, Madrian & Metrick (NBER, 2001, 'For Better or For Worse'): automatic enrolment lifted participation above 85%, but about 80% initially accepted a low default rate and a conservative default fund and half were still there after three years, so the effects on wealth were 'roughly offsetting on average'. For an invested-by-default policy this means the choice of default level matters as much as having one. source
  • A CPPI floor is not a guarantee. Cont & Tankov (2009): with smoothly moving prices the strategy has no downside risk, but 'in real markets this risk is nonnegligible and grows with the multiplier value' - a gap down between rebalancing points (an earnings shock, an overnight fall) can take the portfolio through the floor. The researcher described the floor as a level 'you refuse to go below'. source
  • Independent challenge to the trend-following evidence, which otherwise comes almost entirely from AQR (a seller of the strategy). Huang, Li, Wang & Zhou (2020): market-by-market tests 'reveal little evidence' of the trend effect in or out of sample, and although the strategy made money it performed about the same as a similar strategy that needs no predictability at all. source
  • Trading costs erase most volatility-targeting gains. Barroso & Detzel (2021): after transaction costs, volatility management of strategies other than the whole market 'generally produces zero abnormal returns and significantly reduces Sharpe ratios'; the whole-market version survives costs but 'only provides superior performance when sentiment is high'. source

Harvey et al.'s full text says the result is achieved 'by leveraging the portfolio at times of low volatility and scaling down at times of high volatility', and that 'volatility often increases in periods of negative returns, causing positions to be reduced'.
