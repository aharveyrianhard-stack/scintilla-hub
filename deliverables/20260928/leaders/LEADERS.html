Market leaders: how much of the market they were, what they looked like, and the rotation question

STATUS · all three studies ran on real data. 2020 → 2026 uses SPY's own quarterly holdings (the actual index weights, 28 filings, 2019-09-30 → 2026-06-30). 2004 → 2019 is an estimate from FMP market caps of 113 large companies that still exist — shown in a dimmer colour everywhere. Bars to 2026-09-25. Green = up / ahead, red = down / behind; every line is coloured by its own direction.

What it says, in plain words

  1. The market's gains are more concentrated in a handful of names than before 2020 — how much more is not measured yet. In the measured up years 2020–2025 the ten biggest contributors delivered 67.4% of the year's gain in the middle case (all those years' top-10 points added together ÷ all their index points: 61.1%); the single biggest name 15.9%. For 2004–2019 the estimate gives at least 33.9% (pooled 34.9%) and at least 8.5% for the single biggest — floors, because the only companies it can see (113 that still exist) cover just 52.9–71.0% of the index's weight and explain a middle 65.3% of the up years' points. The real pre-2020 figures are higher by an amount this study cannot size. In 2023 the top 10 gave 17.05 of the index's 24.23 points (70.4%); in 2024 15.70 of 23.31 (67.4%), and one name — NVDA — gave 5.22 points on its own. 2026 so far: 8.68 of 13.12 points (66.2%), led by AAPL, NVDA, MU.
  2. In down years leaders do not carry the index. In 2008 (-38.5%) the best twenty together added 0.02 points; in 2022 (-19.4%) 2.31. Leadership is an up-market phenomenon. The names that were a top-5 contributor most often: AAPL 16×, MSFT 14×, GOOGL 11×, XOM 8×, AMZN 8×, NVDA 6×, CVX 4×, META 4×.
  3. On the last close before their year, leaders did not look special on the chart. The two pools have very different base rates (20.1% of the pre-2020 survivor list led, 9.1% of the full 2020+ index), so each is read on its own. Cloud order barely moved the odds in either: before 2020 bull order 19.5%, mixed 20.1%, bear order 21.8% (base 20.1%); 2020+ 10.4% / 8.2% / 8.3% (base 9.1%). RSI percentile in the stock's whole prior history: 44.9 vs 49.0 before 2020, 51.9 vs 50.2 after. Relative strength against SPY over the prior year: no gap before 2020 (+2.7% vs +2.3%), a clear gap after (+10.5% vs -5.1%); the top tenth by strength led 23.0% of the time before 2020 (base 20.1%) and 20.5% after (base 9.1%).
  4. What did recur: size. Growth only a little, where it can be measured fairly. A leader's starting index weight was 0.80% in the middle case against 0.20% — contribution is weight × return, so the giants lead by arithmetic. Growth is only on equal terms before 2020 (every company in that pool has statements): there, a growing top line lifted the chance of leading from 18.6% to 20.6% for all names and from 25.4% to 27.5% among comparable sizes — under 2.5 points. After 2020 statements exist for 97.0% of leader-years but only 51.0% of the others (mostly names that led in some other year), so the bigger 2020+ gap (13.8% → 25.1%) is not a fair comparison.
  5. Their runs started from washed-out lows — but so did everyone's. Looking back, a leader's run began at a low about 8.8 months before its year, typically 23.8% below its 52-week high, with RSI in the bottom 6.9% of its whole prior history and price below all four cloud lines 71.4% of the time. Companies of the same size that did NOT lead looked the same at their lows (20.8% below the high, RSI percentile 5.4, below all lines 73.9%). The difference is what came after: leaders rose 81.0% to their peak in the middle case, the others 44.2%; revenue growth at the low was 7.8% vs 5.3%.
  6. The rotation question: moving into ONE calmer name trimmed the pain a little and gave up a little return. The "half the pain" came from spreading the money, not from calm. Across 15,736 up-swings in 103 leader names (each swing taken on the day it could first be known), one year later the middle case was: hold the leader +11.2%, one calm strong-trend name +9.2%, the basket of all calm names +11.6%, SPY +12.3%. The worst drop inside that year: hold -20.5%, one calm name -17.5%, the basket -10.4%, SPY -10.3% (1 in 10 worse than -42.7% / -36.1% / -27.3% / -26.7%). Event by event, one calm name's worst drop was 2.4 points shallower than the leader's in the middle case, its return -2.4 points against holding, and it came out ahead 46.7% of the time. The control — one strong-trend name of ANY volatility — behaved like holding (+10.9%, worst drop -21.3%). The calm basket held about 27 names and behaved almost exactly like SPY: in practice "switch to all calm strong-trend names" meant "go back to the index".
  7. After the very biggest swings (the top tenth of a name's own history) holding did a little better: at one year the basket was -3.51 pts against holding (ahead 44.1% of the time) and one calm name -5.30 pts (ahead 43.1%) — momentum persisted a little. In the other nine tenths one calm name trailed by 1.0–3.1 points in the middle case.

How far to trust the two regimes

  • Measured years check out against the index. Quarter by quarter, the weight × return of every company adds up to within 0.62 points of the S&P 500's own quarterly return (the rest is companies that joined or left mid-quarter). 99.1%+ of the weight is priced in every quarter.
  • The estimate was tested on the measured years. Using the estimate method on 2020–2025 gives a top-10 sum +0.61, +1.06, +0.10, +1.19, +1.47, +0.71 points away from the measured answer, and the same top names. It runs slightly high, because FMP caps are full caps while the index uses float. The scale factor (index value ÷ level) moved only between 8.28 and 8.73 billion across 2019–2026.
  • The estimate only sees survivors, so every pre-2020 top-N figure is a floor. Before 2020 only the 113 companies with cap histories here are counted (today's large names, while they were members). They hold 52.9–71.0% of the index's estimated weight (full caps, so the true share is lower still) and explain only part of each year's points — in 2004 2.94 of 8.99, in 2006 8.02 of 13.62 (table below). A company that led and later disappeared (AIG, HP, Yahoo, Lehman…) is simply missing, and a top-N picked from part of the index can only be smaller than the true top-N. The backtest above cannot catch this: after 2020 the survivor list contains the top names by construction, so it measures only the full-cap-vs-float error. The direction of the rise is likely; its size before 2020 is not measured.

Looking back: where the runs began (hindsight, described not selected)

For each leader: the lowest low between the start of the year before and its highest close in its leadership year. The same rule applied to every company of comparable size that did not lead gives the yardstick. The lows look alike; what separated the two groups came after the low.

3 · The rotation question: hold the leader after a big up-swing, or move to a calmer name in a strong trend?

Alan's words: "the market is more bullish than bearish — better to be long-leaning; if I take profits on a volatile name, shift the money into another good one with a lesser risk profile." This is a research question, not advice. The test: every up-swing of every name that was ever a top-20 contributor, taken as it could be known at the time — each pivot high (10 bars each side) is confirmed 10 sessions later and paired with the latest pivot low before it; a later, higher high never removes or moves an earlier event. Both paths start at the close of the confirmation day. Hold = stay in the name. Calm candidates = every other company in the cache that, on that same day, had the Station bull order with price above all four lines AND had moved less than the name over the same swing (about 27 names in the middle case; the leader's yearly volatility 23.2%, theirs 16.8%). Four ways to move the money: one calm name (a single candidate, every candidate counted with equal weight so each event counts once), one strong-trend name of any volatility (the control), the basket of all calm names (equal weight), and SPY.

Reading it: a single calm name took a little less pain than the leader and gave up a little return; a single strong-trend name of any volatility looked like the leader; only spreading across all calm names (or buying SPY) halved the pain. Most of the basket's smoother ride is diversification.

The RSI percentile here reads the stock's whole prior history (expanding, at least 250 earlier readings) instead of S9's rolling 3 years.

What could be wrong

  • Hindsight in the leader list. Parts 2 and 3 study names because they became leaders. Holding them looks better than holding a random stock would have; the rotation result is, if anything, flattering to "hold".
  • Survivors. The switch candidates and the pre-2020 cap list are today's companies. Names that collapsed are missing from both — which also makes every pre-2020 concentration figure a floor.
  • Pooled chances mix two pools. The trait charts and the pooled tables combine a survivor list (base rate 20.1%) with the whole index (9.1%); read the split table. The 2020+ growth numbers compare leaders that almost all have statements against others that mostly do not.
  • One calm name is an average over every candidate, not a chosen stock. Picking the "best" calm name (by trend strength, by quality) was not tested.
  • The chart API's META history was a different security before 9 Jun 2022 (a fund that used the ticker, with a 4-month gap). It was replaced here with FMP's daily prices for Facebook/Meta. The same rule (drop history before any gap of more than 20 days) removed older, reused-ticker history for WM (Washington Mutual), MS (pre-2006), BNY, SPCX, SHAZ and others. This is a data fault in the chart API worth fixing at the source.
  • Overlap. Up-swings in different names often end in the same weeks (the whole market turns), so the 15,736 events are far fewer independent observations than they look.
  • Price only. No dividends, no costs, no taxes. Switching every time a swing completes would trade a lot; taxes alone could swamp a 1-point difference.
  • Three companies' statements came back too small to file (PLTR, SNDK, CRWD) and six leaders have no bars in the cache (PYPL, ACN, CI, VLO, MCK, HES), so they are missing from parts 2–3.

What was not done

  • No full reconstruction of S&P membership and float before 2019 (would need every member's caps, including dead companies — a keyed bulk job).
  • No sector or theme split of the leaders, no valuation (P/E) at the start, no analyst-estimate revisions.
  • No statements fetched for the whole 2020+ pool (about 500 companies), so growth after 2020 is not compared on equal terms.
  • The switch basket is not optimised (no ranking by trend strength or by volatility); it is simply "every calmer name in bull order that day".
  • Nothing was deployed, no database was touched, no trade was made.
