Bottoms: what happens around the lows, the 200-day as a fact, and the rising tide

Statistics lane · 28 Sep 2026 · daily bars to the 25 Sep close · research questions, not buy rules · every chart is a saved file in charts/ next to this page

STATUS · done for SPY, QQQ and IWM, the 200-day test on 59 instruments (indexes, sectors, each year's top 20 at the time), and the tide on all 486 served names. Revised after review, 28 Sep: no-data lows kept apart, one shared set of lows for ranking, data faults cut, point-in-time top 20, still-open stretches counted, and a random-walk yardstick for the 200-day arithmetic. Every swing uses the S9 pivot rule (a low lower than the 10 bars on each side). Every "how extreme" is an own-history percentile — today against every earlier day of the same series, never a trailing window. No fixed look-ahead windows: outcomes run to the next swing or to the close back above the 200-day. Up = green, down = red on every line.

What we found, in plain words

  1. The VIX does not lag the bottom — it turns with it or just before it. At SPY's 126 swing lows since 2003, the VIX's own swing high (same 10-bar pivot rule) came on the same session in 59, before in 42 and after in only 13 (no VIX swing in that stretch: 12). For lows that ended a fall of 10% or more: before or same day in 26 of 28. QQQ and IWM look the same (charts below).
  2. Put/call usually turns first; credit is the slowest. Counting only lows where the series already existed: the put/call ratio (PCC) peaked before the price low at 21 of the 27 deep lows it has a reading for. Over the 104 SPY lows with a credit reading, the HYG÷LQD trough came after the price low at 30 and there was no credit swing at all at 17; 22 more lows came before the credit data starts (Apr 2007) and are shown apart as "no data yet". At the deep lows credit turned before or with price at 23 of 27. SPY's own RSI bottomed before price at 63 of 125 lows — but RSI is made from the same closes, so that describes the fall itself (a slightly lower low on weaker momentum), not an outside confirmation.
  3. Every signal cries wolf — measured. A random SPY day has a 24% chance that a swing low follows within 10 bars. With the VIX at or above its 90th own percentile the chance is 37%; 35% of those VIX episodes were false alarms (no low followed); and 72% of all lows happened WITHOUT it. It is a deep-low detector, not an any-low detector: it was on before 82% of the 10%+ lows.
  4. Companions to the VIX, ranked only among OUTSIDE information and on one shared set of lows (98 SPY lows from 2008-04-08, when every series has a usable reading; 24 of them after 10%+ falls; any day 23%). At the 90th own percentile: VIX alone 35% of days followed by a low, 83% of the 10%+ lows caught. Pairs at the 80th: VIX + put/call 52% / 88% (false alarms 38% of episodes); VIX + credit 34% / 46%. The equity put/call ratio is the strongest outside companion; credit adds the least.
  5. RSI, the distance below the 200-day, volume and breadth describe the fall; they do not confirm it. They are computed from the same prices whose fall defines each low, so "RSI at its 90th stress level caught every 10%+ low" (100%, 65% of days followed by a low) follows almost automatically — a 10% fall pushes RSI into its bottom decile. They are listed apart in the tables. All four together (VIX, put/call, RSI, breadth, each at its 80th level, counted from 2007-10-31, when all four exist): 62% of days followed by a low, 32% of episodes false alarms, 85% of the 26 10%+ lows caught — on the shared set 83%, the same as the VIX alone (83%).
  6. VIX above VIX3M (inverted curve) is a yes/no condition, no threshold to choose: 553 SPY days since mid-2006, 46% followed by a low within 10 bars; 48% of inversion episodes had no low follow; it was on before 49% of the lows it could see and 78% of the 10%+ lows.
  7. Deep below the 200-day: the further fall is bigger than arithmetic explains; the long wait is not. Pooling 4 index funds, 11 sectors + SMH and each year's 20 largest US stocks AT THE TIME (41 names since 2007), days in the deepest 5% of their own history went on to fall a further 12% (median, still-open stretches counted as lower bounds) before a close back above the 200-day — against 6.3% for a random walk started from the same closes, and 3.6% for any day below the 200-day. They took a median 88 sessions to get back, but the same random walk takes 105: the long wait is what distance alone produces (deep days actually got back a little FASTER than chance). Beyond the random walk, deeper days still fell a bit further (rank link -0.10, rotations -0.07 … 0.04) — consistent with a real effect, but a small one. The same deep days had the biggest median rise to the next swing high: 8.1% vs 4.1% for any day.
  8. Picking stocks by today's size flatters them. With today's top 20 (the survivors) the deepest days fell 11% further and bounced 9.8%; with the top 20 of each year at the time, 13.5% and 8.8%.
  9. The rising tide is real and scales with the day. On a median SPY up day 70% of the 486 names rise; on the biggest 5% of SPY days it is 92% (up) and 93% (down). Over whole SPY swings the tide is stronger: a median 87% of names rose over an up-swing, 100% of the sectors. Dispersion peaks AT lows (median 72nd percentile on the low day; 95th at 10%+ lows) and is ordinary at tops (51st).
  10. Today's tide (25 Sep close) is narrow. SPY closed 0.8% below its highest close, yet only 36% of names are above their 50-day (19th percentile of their own history), only 49% of names are above where they were at SPY's last swing low (2026-07-29), and equal-weight (RSP) has lagged SPY by 7.5 points since that low. Research reading, not a call.

2 · "Deep below the 200-day is a problem" — tested as a fact

Pool: SPY, QQQ, IWM, DIA · the 11 SPDR sector funds + SMH · each calendar year's 20 largest US companies by market cap on its first trading day (FMP historical market caps from Nov 2006, 114 large names checked; a stock's days count only in the years it was in that year's top 20 — list below). Today's top 20 (FMP, 28 Sep: NVDA, AAPL, GOOGL, MSFT, AMZN, META, AVGO, TSLA, MU, LLY, BRK-B, AMD, JPM, WMT, V, XOM, JNJ, INTC, MA, ABBV) is shown only as a separate "survivors" line: every one of them recovered from every past drawdown by construction. Daily split-adjusted bars from the chart API. For every day, the distance to the 200-day average is turned into that instrument's own-history percentile (1 = the deepest below it had ever been up to then). Outcomes need no fixed window: the move to the next confirmed swing high, the worst fall before it, and — for days below the 200-day — how much further price fell and how many sessions it took before a close back above the 200-day.

Still-open stretches: 420 below-200 days (pooled) belong to stretches that are still below the 200-day today — the longest waits. They are NOT dropped: they enter the medians as "at least this long / at least this deep" through a survival (Kaplan–Meier) median. Bands: 90% bootstrap, resampling whole stretches above/below the 200-day (the independent units), 150 draws. Rotation test: within each instrument the percentile series is rotated by a random amount against its outcomes, 400 times; it only rules out "no link at all".

How much of it is just arithmetic?

A close further below the average must travel further to get back above it, so a longer wait and a deeper further fall follow even for a coin-flip market. To measure that part, every day below the 200-day was given 20 random futures made of 20-day blocks of that instrument's own daily returns (its own drift and swings), starting from the real 200 closes up to that day. The dashed lines are those random walks; the solid lines are what happened. What could be wrong

  • Survivorship in breadth. The tide and breadth use TODAY's 486 names back-filled to 2003. Names that failed or left are missing, so past breadth reads a little healthier than it was, and the early years carry about 218 names, not 486.
  • Survivorship in the 200-day stocks — reduced, not gone. The stocks are each year's top 20 at the time, but only from 2007 (market caps start Nov 2006), chosen from 114 large names another lane pulled (a name that was top-20 but never on that list would be missed), with AMD's cap estimated from shares × price. Today's-top-20 numbers are shown beside them to make the bias visible.
  • Data faults were cut, not repaired (except META). 77 histories lost their early part; a genuine one-day crash beyond −50% is cut the same way as a ticker change.
  • The random-walk null draws return blocks from each instrument's WHOLE history (including after the day), so it is a yardstick for the arithmetic, not a forecast; 20 futures per day make each day's yardstick noisy, which the pooled medians smooth out.
  • Price-derived conditions overlap the definition of a low. RSI, distance below the 200-day, volume and breadth are shown apart; their catch rates describe the fall, not an outside signal.
  • QQQ starts in 2011. The cached QQQ history has a gap of more than a year; the S9 rule analyses only what follows it, so QQQ misses 2003–2010 (and 2008).
  • Short histories for the other gauges. Put/call from Nov 2006, VIX3M from Jul 2006, HYG from Apr 2007, and each own percentile needs a further 250 readings. Lows before a series' first usable reading are now left out of that series' counts (shown as "no data yet"); the shared-lows table compares all conditions on the same 98 lows from 2008-04-08.
  • Swings are known 10 bars late. A low is only confirmed 10 sessions after it; the base rates measure what was TRUE, not what was visible on the day.
  • Few deep lows. 28 SPY lows ended a fall of 10% or more; percentages on those carry wide error.
  • Put/call is one printed number a day and noisy; its "turn" uses the same pivot rule on that single number.
  • Few deep lows on the shared set. 24 deep lows since 2008: one low moves a catch rate by about 4 points.
  • Overlap in the 200-day pool. SPY, DIA, the sectors and the megacaps move together; the pooled bands resample stretches within each instrument, not across them, so the pooled band is somewhat too narrow. The per-group rows are the safer read.
