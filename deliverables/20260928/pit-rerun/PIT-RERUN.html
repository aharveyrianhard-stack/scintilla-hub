What reversed, and why, in plain words

  1. The "red flags" rule for names under a falling 200-day (stats 3 §3b). On today's names, an entry with no flag went on to fall 33%+ only 7.3% of the time and one with four or five flags about 21.3%: the flags looked like they carried. Counted on every member of the day, the no-flag entries fell 33%+ 13.8% of the time and the four-flag ones 19.4%, with no rise beyond that (five to eight flags: 14.3%, 16.2%, 14.0%, 14.3%). Why, checked on the 2016 → entries: among the members still trading today the flags hardly separate (11.8% fell 33%+ with no flag, 13.9% with four), and the 1,165 entries from names that later left the index fell 33%+ 29.2% of the time with few or many flags alike (24.5% with none, 35.8% with four). The tail lives in the names that left, and the flags do not find it; the old separation came from the Hub's served list, a smaller and more volatile set of names on which the flags happened to sort. The marks at entry (a wide usual day, a deep fall from the high, a 200-day falling faster, a weak market) still separate the bad decile; what does not survive is turning them into a count that sorts the tail out of sample. The scout-tier "avoid list at three or more own flags" should not be built on the old numbers.
  2. The worst fallers' rebound after a low (stats 3 §4). On today's names the tenth of stocks that had fallen most into a SPY low went on to beat SPY by +3.43 points in a quarter, +7.95 in half a year and +11.67 in a year. Drawn from the members on the day of the low, the same basket gives +1.94, −0.10 and +0.98, and only the quarter keeps a range above zero. Why: at the October 2008 low the worst ten fallers among the members were AIG, Morgan Stanley, Genworth, AK Steel, XL, Wachovia, Hartford, Constellation, National Oilwell and US Steel, and 30 of that day's 48 fallers later stopped trading (bought, merged or bankrupt); at the July 2008 low the list was led by Freddie Mac and Fannie Mae. A list of today's names only holds the fallers that came back. The leaders' quarter-long lag itself holds (it is a little clearer, −0.67 points), so the explanation moves: the leaders trail a cap-weighted index for a quarter, and the beaten names' bounce explains part of it for a quarter, not for a year. The rules that add a sleeve of the beaten names drop from luck-proof to leaning.
  3. Small caps under a falling 200-day (stats 3 §3c) and the size cells of the sigma study (§5c). Both were typed by today's size. A company that is small today is small because it fell, so "small caps in the state fell 33%+ 64.3% of the time" becomes 26.1% once each entry is typed by the size the company had then; and the sigma-day cells "mega caps lead after a sigma day, small caps lag" (+0.47 and −6.95 points, both luck-proof) become +0.01 and +0.48, neither shown. The same artefact N9 found in §1d, in two more places.

What held, in one breath. The one-in-ten line under a falling 200-day and what marks it at entry; that the state does not disqualify a name from winning its year; that leaders trail SPY for a quarter after a low and that moving to equal weight is a wash; that a one-sided sigma day is followed by more sigma days; that a name's own sigma day is not a trigger; and the whole rotation study (spreading the money halves the pain and costs a little, one calm name is a little less of both, holding wins after the very biggest swings). Two readings got stronger: leaders' lag after lows (p 0.06 → 0.01) and the strict one-sided sell day (no member up beyond its usual day, 2% or more down), which now clears the false-discovery check at every threshold with SPY +2.34% to +2.97% over the next month against +1.40% on any day; on the Hub's own served-name count it did not.

3 · USUAL DAY history as data (stats 3 §5) — old vs new

Same method: the Hub's own test — a day fires when the close-to-close move is 2× the spread of the 60 moves before it and at least 1%, or 8% outright — with the same ladder, thresholds, clusters, trigger cells and date-block resampling. The change: the test is replayed on the point-in-time bars of every S&P member on its member days (922 names, 166,239 events; the Hub's stored table holds 478 served names, 126,589 events), so both the day counts (5a, 5b) and the per-name events (5c) come from the members of the day, typed by the cap of the day. In 2004 the point-in-time count measures 442 names; the Hub's table measured 226, because it only knows today's names.

The two universes are not the same list: the Hub's served names include ETFs and names outside the S&P 500. On 23 Sep the Hub counted 0 up / 25 down of 486 (a down share of 5.14%, its 88th percentile); among S&P members that day it was 0 up / 13 down of 503 (2.58%, 75th). The live strip stays the Hub's; this section says what the members' record says.

What could still be biased

  1. The market columns are still the served names' breadth. "Share of names above the 50-day" (a mark and a flag in §1) and the fear gauges come from the stats 3 panel, built on today's names; early years overstate breadth a little.
  2. Sectors are today's profiles. A company with no profile today (most of the names that left) has no sector, so the sector rows in §1c cover 4,343 of 11,972 entries.
  3. Scored to the last print. A buy-out counts its premium as the end value; a bankruptcy counts its last trade; later payouts are not in the data. Both directions are in the numbers above.
  4. Caps before 2008 cover 75–85% of member-days. A member-day without a cap is typed "stock (no cap)" and sits in no size row.
  5. The tranche lines are nominal dollars (as stats 3 used), so the mega row is thin before 2015.
  6. The pool for the year rows is the members on the year's first session; a company that joined mid-year is counted from the next year.
  7. The rotation's candidates need 260 sessions of point-in-time bars, so a company's first year in the index is not a candidate year; and the leaders are still chosen with hindsight.
  8. The Hub's live USUAL DAY strip counts a different list (served names, ETFs included) than §3 here. The two disagree on a given day's share, as the 23 Sep example shows.

  1. Nothing on the list was left out.
  2. The Hub's stored sigma tables were not rewritten and the live USUAL DAY strip is unchanged; §3 here is research only.
  3. No Hub room changed. The stats 3, leaders and point-in-time pages carry dated correction boxes (their numbers were not edited); the studies index carries this page.
