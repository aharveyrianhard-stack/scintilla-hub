A survivorship-free universe: who was really in the S&P 500 each day since 2003, and which of today's conclusions change

Point-in-time · N9 · 28 Sep 2026 · built 2026-09-29 03:23 UTC · research, not rules · bars to 2026-09-25
STATUS · built and used. The member list of the S&P 500 for every trading day since 2 Jan 2003 (967 companies were members at some point), the Nasdaq-100 the same way (303), month-end market caps, and daily prices for 98.1% of all S&P member-days. Three of today's studies were re-run on it and are shown old vs new below. The research copy is on this Mac; the copy in the cloud store (R2) needs one job run by the coordinator — the job is written and tested, not run. Green = up / ahead, red = down / behind.

What changed, in plain words

  1. Size tranches — the big one. The old finding reverses. Typed by today's size, mega caps looked like they beat SPY by +1.01 points a quarter and small caps looked doomed at −15.43. Typed by their size on the day, among real S&P members, mega caps trailed (−0.80) and small members led (+1.74). The old numbers were an artefact: a company is mega today because it rose, small today because it fell. What did not change: inside every size, the "pullback cell" (strong trend, weak momentum) still tracks that size's ordinary day — no size turns it into an edge.
  2. Leaders' share of the gains: the old floor was close to the truth. Before 2020 the ten biggest contributors gave a middle 36.1% of an up year's gain (old floor: at least 33.9%); the single biggest 9.3% (old: at least 8.5%). Since 2020 it is about two-thirds either way (measured 67.4%, this rebuild 69.5%). The conclusion stands and is now a measurement, not a floor: concentration roughly doubled after 2020.
  3. What leaders looked like beforehand: mostly unchanged, one small correction. With every member in the pool (not 113 survivors), the chance of a random member leading is 4.5%, not 20.1%. Cloud order still barely moves the odds. Size is still the trait that recurs. The correction: before 2020 there was a small relative-strength edge (leaders +2.4 vs others +0.3 points against SPY; the old run saw none), and leaders sat nearer their 52-week high (-6.7% vs -9.3%).
  4. Buying in tranches on the leaders basket: same answer, a little sharper. The basket of each year's 20 largest members grew about 9.0% a year since 2007 instead of 9.6% (AIG was in it for 2007–08; Berkshire was not before it joined the index in 2010). Laddering still never beat buying at once on return; at 5% and 7% pullbacks its cost is now clearer; its one benefit — a smaller worst paper loss at shallow 2–3% pullbacks — holds and is now significant at 2% too.

1 · The universe

How the member list is built. FMP publishes today's list and a log of every addition and removal. Starting from today's list, each change is undone going backwards in time: on a change date the added company leaves and the removed one comes back. That gives one row per stretch of membership (1004 stretches for the S&P 500). The count stays between 495 and 507 every day since 2003 (the index holds 500 companies and ~503 share lines). 6 records in the S&P log and 5 in the Nasdaq-100 log do not fit (a company "added" that was never removed, or "removed" twice); they are listed below and left as they are, not fixed silently.

Checked against the real index fund. SPY files its holdings every quarter (28 filings, Sep 2019 → Jun 2026). After matching renamed tickers, the rebuilt list and the fund's holdings differ by 0–18 names per quarter, and nearly all of those are still ticker spellings (FLT→CPAY, FBHS→FBIN, VIAC/PARA→PSKY, LB→BBWI) rather than wrong members. From 2025 the two agree to within one name. Before 2019 there is no holdings file to check against.

2 · Leaders' share of the market's gains — old vs new

Old: 2004–2019 estimated from the 113 large companies that still exist (they covered 52.9–71.0% of the index's weight), so every number was "at least". New: every company that was a member each day, weighted by its full market cap, attributed day by day. The rebuilt index tracks the real S&P 500 within a middle 0.74 points a year (worst 2.26; the real index weights by free float, this by full cap). Before 2008 fewer member-days carry a cap (75.4% in 2004, rising to 98.8% by 2019).

What could be wrong

  1. FMP's change log is the only source for who was in. It agrees with SPY's own filings from 2019 on; before 2019 nothing independent was checked. 11 records do not fit and are listed above.
  2. Before 2008 fewer member-days carry a cap (75–85%), so early years lean on the companies FMP still carries. The rebuilt index still tracks the real one within about 1–1.5 points a year.
  3. Full caps, not free float. The real index weights by float; a few family-controlled giants weigh a little more here.
  4. Price repairs are rules, not a data feed. 28 one-day jumps were treated as splits; a real crash on a day the caps cannot confirm would be missed (three known ones are kept by name). 26 names lose history before a long gap.
  5. Delisted names are scored to their last trade. A buy-out at a premium or a bankruptcy's last print is the end value; later payouts are not in the data.
  6. Tranche lines are nominal dollars (as stats 3 used): $200bn was rarer in 2008 than in 2025, so the mega tranche is thin early.
  7. The Nasdaq-100 count runs 101–110: its log is missing a few removals, so it is built but not used in any study here.
