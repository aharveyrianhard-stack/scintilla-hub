1 · Market states that name themselves (hidden Markov regimes), with today's probability RAN TODAY

STOLEN FROM

hmmlearn · Hamilton (1989) regime switching, as in statsmodels' Markov regression notebook · Kritzman, Page & Turkington (2012), Regime shifts: implications for dynamic strategies, FAJ · QuantStart, Market regime detection using hidden Markov models

The question. Do two or three market states fall out of the S&P's own returns and the VIX without anyone drawing a line, how long does each last, and which state are we in today, using only bars up to today?

Data we have. ^GSPC 1927→, ^VIX 1990→, ^VIX3M 2006→, SPY and RSP 2003→ — all in the chart-API cache already.

4 · Volatility-managed sizing: scale exposure by recent volatility, not by conviction

Moreira & Muir (2017), Volatility-Managed Portfolios, J. Finance (NBER w22208) · Harvey, Hoyle, Korgaonkar, Rattray, Sargaison & van Hemert (2018), The impact of volatility targeting

The question. 2 · Study 1 · The market's own states, and today's reading

The question. Let the data name the states. A hidden Markov model is told only that there are two (or three) hidden states, each with its own typical daily return and typical wildness, and that the market stays in a state for a while before switching. It works out from the bars alone which state each day most likely belongs to. Nothing is drawn by hand: no RSI line, no 200-day, no VIX level chosen by eye.

Today, 2026-09-25: CALM. The two-state model on the S&P 500 and the VIX (1990 →) puts today at 1.00 CALM / 0.00 STRESS with parameters frozen at end-2007 (walk-forward), and 1.00 / 0.00 with the whole record's parameters. The three-state version reads CALM 0.01 · MIDDLE 0.99 · STRESS 0.00: today sits in the middle state, whose typical VIX is 17.8. The model that also sees the equal-weight spread and the VIX curve (2006 →) reads CALM 1.00 · MIDDLE 0.00 · STRESS 0.00. The returns-only model on the S&P since 1928 agrees: CALM 0.99.

  1. The states are real, and they are about wildness, not direction. Out of sample (2008 → 2026, parameters from 1990–2007), the 20 sessions after a STRESS day ran at a realised volatility of 18.1% a year against 10.6% after a CALM day: a gap of 10 points with a 90% range of 7 to 12, which never touches zero. LUCK-PROOF on wildness. On direction the two states cannot be told apart: the median next-20 return after STRESS was +2.08% against +1.13% after CALM, and the difference in means has a 90% range of -0.4 to +1.2 points (p 0.39). The 1928 → model says the same (10 to 14 vol points, return range -0.4 to +1.0, p 0.48). NOT SHOWN on direction.
  2. How long a state lasts. With the VIX in the model, a CALM stretch is expected to last 94 sessions and a STRESS stretch 75 (out of sample the middle CALM run was 22 sessions, the longest tenth over 104; STRESS 12 and 83). The typical VIX inside CALM is 14.3, inside STRESS 24.6. Since 2008 there were 58 STRESS stretches: that is the count of independent episodes behind every number here, not the 47.4% of days.
  3. What the state adds to the 200-day. Out of sample, above the 200-day: a CALM day was followed by a next-20 median of +1.12% with a bad-case (10th percentile) of -3.30%; a STRESS day above the 200-day by +2.16% and -4.53%. Below the 200-day almost every day is STRESS (1044 of 1090), with the bad case at -8.47%. So the state is a second label that mostly sorts the above-200-day days into quiet and jumpy ones; the 200-day side still does the heavy lifting on the downside.
  4. The STRESS state has not paid to avoid. In every model, the average return after STRESS days was as good as or better than after CALM days (the 2006 → model: +2.86% vs +1.50%, difference range +0.4 to +2.7). That is the rising-tide fact seen from another side: stressed days cluster in falls, and falls have been bought. What the state does tell you is how big the next month's swings will be, which is what position size should answer to (proposal 4).
  5. Volatility itself changed level 63 times since 1990 when a change means a 40% shift held for 60 sessions (85 times at 25%). The current stretch began 2025-10-10 at a median 13.0% a year; today's 20-day figure is 10.8%.

3 · Study 2 · When each sector's trend last changed, and this week's rotation graph of the cohorts

The question. The sector-rotation study measured relative strength over fixed windows (16, 72 and 646 sessions) and found that a sector's rank tells you almost nothing about its next rank at a quarter's range. This study lets a change-point search (PELT, the standard offline method in ruptures) decide where each sector's pace against SPY changed, with no window chosen by hand, and then asks the only question that matters for trading: when the search has just found a new stretch, does the new pace carry on? The second half puts Alan's 20 cohorts on a relative rotation graph, the JdK picture every sector desk uses, under the public approximation of its formula.

  1. Where the sectors stand, dated. 3 of 11 sectors are in a stretch that beats SPY: TECH since 2026-08-07 (+52% a year vs SPY), HEALTH CARE since 2026-05-15 (+34% a year vs SPY), ENERGY since 2026-06-05 (+24% a year vs SPY). The newest changes are TECH (2026-08-07, +52%/yr), INDUSTRIALS (2026-07-31, -65%/yr), UTILITIES (2026-07-31, -116%/yr). A stretch that is only a few weeks old is the one most likely to be redrawn as weeks arrive; the table gives each sector's change count under two penalty strengths so you can see which changes are robust.
  2. A fresh change is rarely visible in time, and when it is, it carries little forward. On a grid of every fourth week since 2008 (2423 sector-weeks, each search seeing only the last ten years of bars to that week), the online search flagged only 35 sector-weeks where a change under 8 weeks old had just been found: the search needs weeks of evidence, so by the time it speaks most of the move has happened. Both flag sets are under 20 episodes, so the standard calls them A LIST. When the new stretch was stronger than the last, the sector's next 13 weeks against SPY averaged +0.7 pts (n 15, 33.3% positive, 90% range -4.9 to +6.6); when weaker, -2.8 pts (n 20, 45.0% positive, 90% range -6.5 to +0.5); any sector on any week: -0.6 pts (range -0.8 to -0.4). Read the chart: the two colours' ranges overlap each other and the any-week line, so a detected change is a description of the past, not a forecast. 4 · Study 3 · A Reality Check over every rule family we have published

The question. Our studies searched families of rules: 100 RSI percentile rungs, RSI zones on either side of the 200-day, bands of distance to the 200-day, VIX levels and the VIX curve, calendar months. Every family reports its best member. The Reality Check (White 2000) and its sharper form, the SPA test (Hansen 2005), ask one thing: if every rule were pure luck, how often would the best of them look this good? Sullivan, Timmermann and White asked it of 7,846 rules on the Dow in 1999 and found the best rule's 100-year record vanished after 1986. Here it is asked of 480 rules per instrument, on five instruments, with runs of days resampled (block 60).

A rule = be long the index for 5, 20 or 60 sessions after its condition holds at a close, flat otherwise. Its yardstick is being long the same share of days without any condition, the "any day" line. "Excess" is the rule's mean return over that yardstick, in points a year. Each family's best rule is shown with its own p-value (as if it were the only rule tried) beside its SPA p-value (the whole family's search counted), the StepM list of rules that survive at 10%, and a walk-forward: the rule the first half would have picked, and what it earned on the second.

  1. On the funds we trade (SPY, QQQ, IWM, DIA, 2003 →), nothing survives. Family by family, the best rule's SPA p-value runs from 0.21 to 0.95; over the union of every rule searched: SPY 0.66, QQQ 0.50, IWM 0.95, DIA 0.38. StepM keeps no rule. On SPY the best of all 480 rules (RSI pct rung 18 · hold 60) earned +3.6 points a year over any day and its own p-value, taken alone, is 0.014: that is exactly how the best of 480 coin flips looks. Chart C2 makes the point: the best SPY rung sits at +3.0 points a year while the best of 100 rungs by luck alone reaches +4.0. The slope across rungs (low rungs better) is ρ = -0.28 with p 0.41: a lean, not a fact.
  2. On the S&P index since 1928, three things survive the search count. (a) Being above the 200-day (> 5% above 200d · hold 60: +3.0 points a year, SPA p 0.026); StepM keeps 6 of the band rules, all of them "above". The first half (1928–1976) would have picked the same rule and it earned +2.2 points a year on 1977–2026, range +0.4 to +4.0. LUCK-PROOF. This is the trend-following fact the public work reports (Faber; Hurst, Ooi & Pedersen), not a Scintilla finding. (b) Skip September (+1.5 points a year, SPA p 0.044; walk-forward +1.2, range +0.4 to +2.1). LUCK-PROOF over 98 years, though the regime study already showed the last 30 years' September range includes zero, so its size today is unsure. (c) A dip in RSI while above the 200-day (RSI bottom 30% & above 200d · hold 20: +3.0 points a year, SPA p 0.009, StepM keeps 3 rules of that shape) — but the first half would have picked a different member of the family (RSI top 5% & above 200d · hold 60) and that one earned -0.1 on the second half. Over the union of all 480 rules the SPA p is 0.083 and StepM keeps RSI bottom 30% & above 200d · hold 20 and > 5% above 200d · hold 60. LEANING for the RSI-dip-in-uptrend rule, which is the S8 headline re-read: it is real on the century record, it is not visible on 23 years of SPY.
  3. The RSI rungs themselves never survive, on any instrument, in any era: best-rung SPA p 0.83 on the 1928 record, 0.57 on SPY. The statistician's reading stands: a rung is not a finding; at most the slope is, and the slope is a lean.
  4. The walk-forward chart is the plainest picture. Of 17 family-instrument pairs, the rule the first half would have picked cleared zero on the second half in 2: the two on the century record. Every fund rule picked on 2003–2014 fell back to the any-day line on 2015–2026.

What could be wrong
(1) Hidden Markov fits can settle in a local optimum: eight random starts were tried and the best likelihood kept, and the states are named by volatility so they cannot swap labels; still, the three-state 1990 → model's out-of-sample table shows a MIDDLE state that behaves differently from its in-sample twin, which is that weakness showing. (2) The cohort indexes are equal weights of today's members: their history is a survivor's history, so the cohort tails should be read with that in mind; this week's position is unaffected. (3) The change-point penalty is a choice; it is shown at two strengths and stated in plain terms (a 40% shift in volatility held 60 sessions; the library's BIC rule for the sectors). (4) The Reality Check counts the rules this page built, 480 per instrument; the studies searched more (S7's 178 and S7b's 254 rules are not here because their per-trade series are not in the repo). A wider search can only raise the p-values. (5) The RRG formula is the public approximation; JdK's constants are proprietary, so quadrants near a line may differ from a StockCharts screen. (6) Nothing here is a trading rule; every "excess" is close to close with no costs.
What was not done
No deploy, no push to production, no database write, no fetch. Proposals 4–10 are designed, not run. The S7/S7b data-snooping audit (statistician's N6) still needs the per-trade export.
